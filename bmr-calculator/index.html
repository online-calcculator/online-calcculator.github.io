The number is a starting point for understanding energy expenditure, not a prescription for how many calories to eat.

How to Use the BMR Calculator

Choose U.S., metric, or other units, then enter your age, select the available male/female formula category, and provide your height and weight. The reference calculator shows an age range of 15–80; its standard equations were developed largely for adults, so a result for a teenager requires extra caution. Check that feet and inches, centimeters, pounds, or kilograms are in the intended fields.

Open Settings if you want results in kilojoules instead of Calories, or want to switch from Mifflin–St Jeor to Revised Harris–Benedict or Katch–McArdle. Katch–McArdle needs a body-fat percentage as an additional input. Use an estimate only if you understand how uncertain your body-fat measurement may be.

After calculation, read the main BMR value first, then the activity-based daily calorie estimates. A result displayed as “Calories/day” means dietary kilocalories per day (kcal/day); 1 kcal equals 4.184 kJ. Recalculate when your body weight or other relevant inputs change. What Your BMR Result Actually Tells You

A result of 1,780 Calories/day means the selected equation predicts about 1,780 kilocalories of energy use over 24 hours under resting or basal-like conditions. It does not mean you must eat exactly 1,780 calories, that you burn only 1,780 calories on an ordinary day, or that eating below this number is automatically unsafe or appropriate.

True basal metabolic rate is measured under strict, controlled conditions, typically after an overnight fast and substantial rest in a thermally neutral setting. Resting metabolic rate (RMR), also called resting energy expenditure (REE), uses less restrictive measurement conditions. Many popular “BMR” prediction equations, including Mifflin–St Jeor, were developed to predict REE. In everyday calculator use, the terms are often loosely interchanged, but they are not identical laboratory measurements.

Total daily energy expenditure (TDEE) adds energy used for movement, exercise, digestion, and other daily demands. Consequently, an estimated maintenance-calorie number is usually above BMR. Neither output is a directly measured personal value.

Three Equations Explained

All three methods return a daily energy estimate, but they use different inputs and statistical assumptions. For the first two methods, W is weight in kilograms, H is height in centimeters, and A is age in years. The equation’s male/female category reflects how its original data were modeled; it does not capture every individual's physiology, body composition, or hormone status.

Mifflin–St Jeor: the practical default

For men: BMR estimate = 10W + 6.25H − 5A + 5. For women: BMR estimate = 10W + 6.25H − 5A − 161. The original research predicted resting energy expenditure, and the commonly used integer coefficients are rounded versions of its fitted equation.

Working example: man, age 30, weight 80 kg, height 180 cm: (10 × 80) + (6.25 × 180) − (5 × 30) + 5 = 800 + 1,125 − 150 + 5 = 1,780 kcal/day. This is the example used in the activity chart above. For the same height, age, and weight, selecting the other sex-specific equation changes the constant term and therefore the estimate.

Mifflin–St Jeor is often a reasonable first method for nonpregnant adults because comparison research found relatively good average performance among commonly used equations. Its apparent precision should not be mistaken for individual accuracy: validation studies still find meaningful errors.

Revised Harris–Benedict: an alternative age–height–weight model

For men: BMR estimate = 13.397W + 4.799H − 5.677A + 88.362. For women: BMR estimate = 9.247W + 3.098H − 4.330A + 447.593. This 1984 revision uses the same types of inputs but different coefficients, so it need not agree with Mifflin–St Jeor.

Worked example: woman, age 35, weight 68 kg, height 165 cm: (9.247 × 68) + (3.098 × 165) − (4.330 × 35) + 447.593 = 628.796 + 511.170 − 151.550 + 447.593 = 1,436.009, or approximately 1,436 kcal/day. A difference between equations reflects model assumptions, not a detected change in your metabolism.

Katch–McArdle: uses estimated lean body mass

Katch–McArdle uses fat-free (lean) body mass instead of age and height. First calculate lean mass in kilograms: LBM = W × (1 − body-fat percentage ÷ 100). Then calculate estimated resting expenditure: 370 + 21.6 × LBM. In the compact expression 370 + 21.6(1 − F)W, F must be a fraction such as 0.20, not the whole number 20.

Working example: 80 kg body weight with 20% estimated body fat: LBM = 80 × (1 − 0.20) = 64 kg; energy estimate = 370 + (21.6 × 64) = 1,752 kcal/day. Because body-fat percentages from scales, handheld devices, and other consumer methods can vary, this formula can become less useful when its lean-mass input is unreliable. A lean-mass equation is not automatically more accurate for everyone.

Which Formula Should You Choose?

Start with Mifflin–St Jeor for a general adult estimate when body-fat percentage is unavailable. Consider Revised Harris–Benedict as a comparison, not as proof that a higher or lower result is correct. Consider Katch–McArdle if you have a reasonably credible lean-mass estimate and want to understand how body composition affects the prediction. The formula you choose is only one source of uncertainty; the activity multiplier may create an even larger difference in estimated daily needs.

No single equation is best for every age, body size, clinical condition, or athletic population. For an individual whose nutrition needs have medical consequences, indirect calorimetry or a clinician-directed assessment may be more useful than choosing whichever online formula gives a preferred answer.

Why BMR Changes and What a Formula Cannot See

Body size and lean tissue: Maintaining a larger body generally requires more resting energy, and fat-free mass is a major determinant of resting expenditure. However, two people with the same measured lean mass may still have different metabolic rates.

Age: Population equations typically predict lower resting energy expenditure at older ages after other inputs are held constant. This is a statistical trend, not an inevitable fixed-rate decline for every person.

Genes, hormones, and medical conditions: Differences in organ size and tissue metabolism, thyroid function, illness, medications, and other physiological influences can change an individual's needs. A basic online form does not measure these effects.

Temperature, recovery, and physiological stress: Cold exposure, fever, sleep-related conditions, injury, and recovery can influence energy expenditure. These effects depend on circumstances and should not be turned into a universal percentage adjustment without individual evidence.

Food intake and body-weight change: Digestion requires energy, but spreading the same food over many small meals is not a reliable way to make resting metabolism meaningfully higher. Sustained energy restriction and weight change can alter energy expenditure, and changing weight can make a previously useful estimate outdated.

Pregnancy, lactation, growth, and training: Calorie requirements may be different during pregnancy, breastfeeding, adolescence, and periods of unusually high training load. This adult-oriented formula page is not an individualized assessment for those situations.

Accuracy: Estimation Is Not Measurement

Prediction equations are developed by fitting averages to study populations. For example, a systematic review found Mifflin–St Jeor more likely than several alternatives to predict measured RMR within 10% in studied adults, but that still leaves substantial individual error. A subsequent validation study found that accuracy was lower in participants with obesity than those without. Do not display a computed number as though it were precise to the last calorie.

Indirect calorimetry estimates metabolic expenditure from measured oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide production under controlled conditions. When accuracy is important, such as some clinical nutrition situations, a qualified professional can assess whether an appropriate measurement is available and how it should be interpreted.

The activity-based figure carries additional uncertainty because daily activity is variable and the selected level is descriptive rather than measured. Comparing a sensible starting estimate with consistent longer-term weight and dietary records can help reveal whether the estimate is useful, without treating day-to-day scale fluctuation as proof of a metabolic change.

Common Interpretation Mistakes

Do not equate BMR with maintenance calories: the first describes rest; the second approximates all-day energy expenditure. Do not treat BMR as a minimum required calorie intake or as a safe weight-loss target. Do not enter body fat as 20 in a formula that expects the fraction 0.20. Do not compare formula results without checking units, rounding, age, and chosen sex-specific coefficients. Do not infer that an exercise category guarantees a particular daily calorie burn.

When to Seek Individualized Guidance

If you are under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, recovering from illness, experiencing unexplained weight changes, managing an eating disorder, or making a medically supervised nutrition plan, a general-purpose BMR calculator may be unsuitable on its own. A registered dietitian or other qualified clinician can interpret energy needs alongside your circumstances. The calculator is an educational estimate, not a diagnostic test.
