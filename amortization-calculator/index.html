How to use the amortization calculator

  1. Enter the amount you borrow, not the full purchase price if you made a down payment.
  2. Specify the loan term in years and months and enter the annual interest rate as a percentage.

The standard calculation assumes a fixed rate and regular monthly payments. If the interface offers optional extra payments, use the loan start date to place those payments on the schedule and specify when each extra amount begins or occurs.

The extra-payment choices shown on the reference interface are an additional monthly amount, a recurring yearly amount, and one or more one-time amounts. These are extra payments toward principal in the illustration below; they are not a replacement for the scheduled payment. Only use features actually available on the live calculator installed on your page.

Read the monthly payment first, then the total of scheduled payments and total interest. Move to the month-by-month and year-by-year schedule to see how balances change. A date is useful for locating a payoff month on a calendar, but it does not by itself change the payment in the simplified fixed-period formula.

What amortization means

Loan amortization is the gradual repayment of borrowed principal through a series of payments. On a conventional fixed-rate, fully amortizing loan, the regular principal-and-interest payment stays approximately level, while its two components change. Interest is calculated on the unpaid balance: a high balance creates more interest early on, leaving less of the payment to reduce debt. As the balance falls, interest gets smaller and more of the same payment goes toward principal.

Principal is the amount still owed on the loan. Interest is the price charged for borrowing that balance. An amortization schedule records both parts separately, making it possible to see why a $1,700 payment does not reduce the balance by $1,700.

The monthly payment formula

For a fixed-rate loan with equal end-of-month payments and monthly periodic interest, the payment is:

M = P × r × (1 + r)^n ÷ [(1 + r)^n − 1]

Here, M is the scheduled monthly principal-and-interest payment; P is the original loan principal; r is the monthly rate (annual nominal rate ÷ 12, written as a decimal); and n is the total number of monthly payments (12 × years + additional months). This formula is for a standard declining-balance installment loan, not for an interest-only or variable-rate contract.

If the annual rate is zero, use M = P ÷ n instead; otherwise the displayed formula has a zero denominator. For a 6% annual rate, r = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005. A 15-year term has 180 scheduled payments.

Worked example: a $200,000, 15-year loan at 6%

Take P = $200,000, r = 0.005, and n = 180. Substituting those values produces M = $1,687.713656…, displayed as $1,687.71 per month. Across 180 payments, the unrounded model gives $303,788.46 paid and $103,788.46 in interest. Small differences of a few cents can arise if a lender rounds every monthly payment or interest charge rather than carrying full precision internally.

Month 1 interest = $200,000 × 0.005 = $1,000.00. Month 1 principal ≈ $1,687.71 − $1,000.00 = $687.71. Ending principal ≈ $199,312.29. In month 2, interest is charged on this smaller balance, so the interest portion drops to about $996.56 while principal rises to about $691.15.

How to read an amortization schedule

Each row represents one payment period. Interest is the cost charged for that period; principal is the amount that reduces the balance; ending balance is what remains after the payment. For a typical fully amortizing schedule, principal plus interest equals the regular payment (plus any principal-directed extra amount if included). Yearly schedules summarize groups of monthly payments; they are not separate annual bills.

Why interest is front-loaded

The early interest share is not an extra upfront charge in this model; it follows directly from charging the same periodic rate against a larger unpaid balance. In the example, the first payment contains $1,000 in interest, whereas the last contains about $8.40. At the same time, the principal portion grows from roughly $687.71 to $1,679.32. This is why looking only at the scheduled monthly amount can hide the time pattern of debt reduction.

How extra payments change the result

A principal-only extra payment immediately lowers the unpaid balance. Future interest is then calculated on that lower amount, so the next regular installment reduces principal faster. If the normal monthly payment remains unchanged, the loan generally ends earlier and costs less in total interest. Ask your servicer how to designate extra amounts as principal payments and whether a prepayment charge or other restriction applies; handling is determined by the loan contract.

Monthly, annual, and one-time extra payments

Monthly extras are useful for a fixed amount that can be paid with every installment. Annual extras can model an occasional bonus or another predictable once-a-year contribution; select the month when it actually reaches the loan. One-time extras describe an isolated principal reduction. If the tool allows several one-time payments, enter each date separately rather than assuming they all occur at the beginning of the loan. Stop an extra-payment schedule once the loan is fully repaid.

Payment reduction versus early payoff

In the early-payoff model used here, extra principal reduces the remaining term rather than automatically recalculating the required scheduled payment. Some lenders may offer a formal recast after a substantial principal payment, which keeps the remaining contractual term but lowers future required payments. A refinance is different: it replaces the old loan with a new contract and can involve a new rate, fees and repayment term. A generic amortization model does not determine whether either option is available.

Loan term and interest rate trade-offs

A longer repayment term usually lowers the monthly principal-and-interest amount but keeps the balance outstanding longer, increasing total interest when the rate is the same. A shorter term raises the required payment but normally reduces lifetime interest. Compare both the payment and the total interest, and consider whether the higher payment leaves adequate room in your budget.

The interest rate used here is the contractual rate for calculating periodic interest. It is not necessarily the annual percentage rate (APR), which may include certain loan costs depending on the product and disclosure rules. If you compare lender offers, also review origination fees, closing costs, payment frequency and whether the rate can change.

What the calculator does and does not, include

This fixed-rate amortization approach models principal, scheduled interest and, when supported, extra principal payments. It does not automatically estimate property taxes, homeowners or mortgage insurance, HOA dues, vehicle registration costs, lender servicing fees, late charges, or the cost of refinancing. A mortgage’s amount actually due each month may therefore exceed the principal-and-interest figure.

The formula also assumes a constant periodic rate and regularly spaced payments. Loans that accrue interest daily, use non-monthly payment intervals, change rates, include payment holidays or capitalization of unpaid interest, or apply fees before principal require different rules. For an actual payoff amount, rely on the lender’s dated payoff statement, not the illustrative balance on a generic schedule.

Fixed-rate loans, credit cards, interest-only loans, and balloons

A standard fixed-rate installment loan is well suited to a declining-balance amortization table. Credit cards are usually revolving accounts with variable payments, so a single fixed monthly payment plan may not represent their actual repayment. During an interest-only period, the borrower typically pays interest without reducing principal. A balloon loan may require a large remaining balance at maturity. Negative amortization happens when scheduled payments fail to cover all accrued interest and the unpaid amount increases what is owed. These arrangements should not be mistaken for a conventional fully amortizing schedule.

Amortization in business accounting is different

The word amortization also describes allocating an asset’s capitalized cost over an accounting or tax period. In financial reporting, it commonly concerns intangible assets; depreciation is the usual term for allocating the cost of tangible assets such as machinery or buildings. Loan amortization is a repayment schedule, whereas accounting amortization is an expense-allocation process. This loan calculator does not compute asset amortization deductions.

Intangible assets and U.S. tax treatment

For U.S. federal tax purposes, qualifying acquired Section 197 intangible assets generally have a 15-year amortization period. Depending on the facts and exceptions, examples may include acquired goodwill, customer relationships, certain trade names and other acquired business intangibles. Not every patent, copyright, software purchase or self-created asset falls under the same rule, and acquired assets may be subject to restrictions such as anti-churning provisions. Tangible business property follows separate depreciation rules. The correct treatment also depends on the asset, transaction and accounting purpose.

Startup costs are another separate rule

Under the IRS 2025 Schedule C instructions, an eligible business starting in 2025 may elect to deduct up to $5,000 in qualifying startup costs, with that immediate deduction reduced when total startup costs exceed $50,000; eligible remaining amounts may be amortized over 180 months beginning when the business starts. Other entity types and years may involve their own instructions. This is a tax topic, not something to model by entering startup costs as a loan into the calculator.

Common mistakes to avoid

Entering purchase price instead of amount borrowed inflates every result. Treating a 6% annual rate as 6.0 per month produces a vastly different payment; the monthly decimal rate in the formula is 0.005. Confusing principal-and-interest with the full mortgage bill understates housing costs. Rounding intermediate values too early makes schedules disagree with lender statements. Assuming an extra payment automatically lowers the required installment confuses early payoff with loan recasting.

Questions borrowers often ask

For a conventional fixed-rate fully amortizing loan, the required principal-and-interest portion is designed to stay about the same, subject to the final rounding adjustment. The amount paid for taxes and insurance may change; variable-rate loans may also change their required principal-and-interest payment.

💡 Pro Tip: Check the starting balance, annual rate, actual payment date, payment-frequency convention, extra-payment application and fees. Some contracts use daily simple interest rather than the equal-month model. Even when the underlying formulas agree, rounding each month to cents can make small differences accumulate.

A schedule based on today’s rate can illustrate one possible path, but it is not a reliable forecast if the rate changes. A variable-rate model needs rate-change dates and rules, recalculated payments where applicable, and a scenario for future interest rates.
