How to Use the Debt-to-Income Ratio Calculator

Enter your gross income before taxes in the income section, then enter the recurring monthly or annual payments for housing, credit cards, auto loans, student loans, and other obligations. Choose Month or Year separately for each amount. Click Calculate to see both DTI ratios, the income breakdown, and an illustrative new-home affordability estimate. Use Clear to remove your entries and start again.

Debt-to-Income Ratio Formula

Debt-to-income (DTI) measures how much of your gross monthly income goes toward regular debt payments. The calculator converts annual amounts to monthly values before applying these formulas:

Back-end DTI (%) = (All monthly housing and debt payments ÷ Gross monthly income) × 100

Front-end DTI (%) = (Monthly housing expenses ÷ Gross monthly income) × 100

Housing expenses in this calculator include rent, mortgage, property tax, homeowner association (HOA) fees, and homeowners insurance. Other debt payments include credit-card minimum payments, student loans, car loans, and other required liabilities. The back-end DTI includes both groups. Enter payments, not outstanding loan balances. If you enter both rent and mortgage, both are counted until you change the entries.

Worked Example

Suppose you earn $60,000 per year, or $5,000 per month, before tax. You pay $1,200 monthly in rent, $200 toward credit cards, and $250 on an auto loan. Monthly obligations total $1,650. Your front-end ratio is $1,200 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 24%. Your back-end DTI is $1,650 ÷ $5,000 × 100 = 33%. This leaves $3,350 of gross monthly income before taxes and other spending not listed here.

Understanding Your DTI Results

The results meter groups back-end ratios at or below 35% as relatively comfortable, 35%–50% as cautionary, and 50% or more as very stressful. These are illustrative bands, not mortgage approval rules. A commonly cited conventional-lending guideline is a 28% front-end ratio and 36% back-end ratio, but actual qualifying limits differ by lender, loan program, and borrower circumstances.

The Income Breakdown chart divides gross income into housing expenses, other debt obligations, and the income remaining after those listed payments. A remaining-income figure is not take-home pay: income taxes, utilities, food, childcare, medical expenses, and most everyday costs are not subtracted.

How the Home Affordability Estimate Works

For a separate illustration, the calculator first estimates the maximum monthly payment for a replacement home as the smaller of 28% of gross monthly income and 36% of gross monthly income minus other recurring debt payments. It then estimates the home price using a fully amortizing loan, a chosen down payment, and annual property tax and insurance assumptions. Expand Home affordability assumptions in the calculator to adjust the interest rate, down payment, mortgage term, and estimated annual carrying costs.

This estimate assumes your current rent or housing costs stop when the new payment begins. It does not deduct closing costs, PMI, HOA dues on the new property, or other loan-specific expenses; actual affordability may therefore differ substantially.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between front-end and back-end DTI?

Front-end DTI includes housing-related payments only. Back-end DTI includes housing plus other recurring debt payments and is the ratio most often meant by “debt-to-income ratio.”

Do I use gross income or take-home pay?

Use income before taxes and deductions. Enter income from multiple sources only when it can reasonably be treated as income; lenders may require proof and may exclude irregular sources.

Is rent always included when applying for a mortgage?

The calculator counts the rent you enter as a housing expense so you can assess your current budget. When underwriting a proposed purchase, a lender may use the expected new housing payment instead of rent that will cease. Avoid counting a replaced housing payment twice.

Does a 36% DTI guarantee loan approval?

No. Underwriting can consider credit history, employment, assets, loan program, property-related expenses, and other requirements. The calculator does not issue a lending decision.

How can I improve my DTI ratio?

Reduce required monthly payments where feasible, avoid taking on additional debt, or increase stable, documentable gross income. Debt repayment should also be weighed against emergency savings and other financial priorities.
