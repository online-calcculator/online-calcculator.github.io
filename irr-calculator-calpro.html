How to Use the IRR Calculator

Choose the section that matches your investment's cash-flow pattern. For a single starting investment and an ending account balance, use fixed cash flow. If the investment receives or pays different amounts from year to year, use irregular cash flow.

Fixed cash flow

  1. Enter the amount invested at the start and the number of whole years and additional months you held the investment.
  2. Enter its remaining balance or ending sale value.
  3. Choose Withdraw when you take money out or Deposit when you contribute additional money. Enter the recurring amount and frequency; use 0 when no recurring cash flows occur.
  4. Choose whether each contribution or withdrawal occurs at the beginning or end of its period, then select Calculate.

The fixed calculator assumes equally spaced payments at the selected frequency. For an incomplete final period, the calculator includes only payments scheduled before or on the investment's end date; the ending balance is recognized at the exact stated holding time.

Irregular annual cash flow

Enter the initial investment as a positive amount. Record later income as positive cash flows and further investments or expenses as negative cash flows, one year at a time. Year 1 is received or paid at the end of the first year. Leave a field blank when no further years are needed; enter 0 to preserve an empty year between two cash flows. Expand the form to record up to 50 years.

IRR Formula and Calculation Method

Internal rate of return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes the net present value (NPV) of an investment's cash flows equal to zero:

0 = CF₀ + CF₁ / (1 + r)¹ + CF₂ / (1 + r)² + … + CFₙ / (1 + r)ⁿ

Here, CF₀ is generally a negative initial outlay; each later CF is a signed net cash flow; r is IRR per period; and n is the number of periods. The calculation uses numerical root-finding rather than a fixed shortcut.

For the irregular calculator, each period is one year, so the output is an annual IRR. For the fixed calculator, amounts are discounted by their precise assumed times in years using (1 + r)t. Thus, the output is an effective annual rate even for monthly, weekly, or other recurring payments.

Worked IRR Examples

Example 1: Fixed monthly withdrawals

Invest $10,000, withdraw $100 at the end of each month for two years and six months, and finish with $15,000. The 30 withdrawals total $3,000, so the dollar profit is $15,000 + $3,000 − $10,000 = $8,000, and the simple gross return is 80%. Discounting each withdrawal at its monthly date and the final balance at year 2.5 gives an effective annual IRR of approximately 29.768%.

Example 2: Uneven annual cash flows

Suppose you invest $50,000 now, contribute an additional $10,000 in year 1, receive $30,000 in year 2, and $50,000 in year 3. The equation is:

0 = −50,000 − 10,000/(1+r) + 30,000/(1+r)² + 50,000/(1+r)³

The answer is an annual IRR of approximately 12.446%. Total contributions are $60,000 and total cash inflows are $80,000, giving a net gain of $20,000 and a simple gross return of 33.333%.

Understanding the Results

IRR accounts for when cash is invested or received. Total Return is the undiscounted dollar gain or loss after additional contributions and withdrawals. Gross Return compares that gain with the total invested capital, without accounting for timing. Therefore IRR and gross return are not interchangeable.

The donut chart separates the initial investment, any later contributions and the investment gain. When the outcome is a loss, the chart displays the magnitude of the loss, rather than a negative slice, and explicitly labels it as such.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good IRR?

It depends on risk, financing costs, opportunity costs, and the minimum return an investor requires. IRR by itself cannot show how large the investment is or how risky future cash flows may be.

Can IRR be negative?

Yes. A negative IRR can arise when the present value of anticipated receipts is below the upfront and subsequent costs. A negative annual IRR is not the same thing as negative cash flow in a particular year.

Why can the same total profit produce different IRRs?

Because IRR reflects timing. Money received earlier is discounted for fewer years and can support a higher IRR even when two projects have the same undiscounted profit.

Can more than one IRR exist?

Yes. If cash flows switch signs more than once, the NPV equation may have multiple valid roots. For detected multiple roots, this tool lists the rates and identifies the one shown in the headline; for financial decisions, compare NPV at an appropriate discount rate instead. No numerical scan can guarantee finding every closely spaced root.

Does this calculator support dates or XIRR?

No. Irregular mode supports uneven amounts at regular annual intervals, not arbitrary calendar dates. A dated XIRR calculation requires a date for each cash flow. Fixed mode makes standard equal-spacing assumptions for the chosen payment frequency.

Why does my spreadsheet show a different number?

Check the signs of cash flows, whether the first payment occurs immediately, the time interval, partial holding periods, and whether the spreadsheet gives a periodic rate rather than an effective annual rate. Some spreadsheet functions select different roots depending on their starting estimate.

Assumptions and Limitations

  • Currency amounts are treated as dollars; no inflation, taxes, fees, or currency conversion are applied unless included in the entered cash flows.
  • Recurring cash flows are equally spaced (52 payments/year for weekly, 26 for biweekly, and 24 for semimonthly). Actual calendar dates are not used.
  • The solver searches a wide but finite range of annual rates. An answer may not exist within that range, and closely spaced or repeated roots might not be detected.
  • IRR does not measure investment risk, overall project size, or a guaranteed reinvestment rate. Compare investments using NPV and other appropriate measures as well.
