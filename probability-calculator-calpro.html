How to Use the Probability Calculator

Choose the section matching your problem. For two independent events, enter P(A) and P(B) as decimals from 0 to 1 and select Calculate. The calculator shows complementary probabilities, the chance both occur, the chance at least one occurs, the chance exactly one occurs, and the chance neither occurs. Each mode has its own results directly above its input form.

To work backward from known event relationships, enter exactly two values in the Probability Solver for Two Events. Some valid pairs do not uniquely determine P(A) and P(B); in those cases, the solver explains the ambiguity rather than guessing.

For a series of independent trials, enter each event probability and the number of repetitions. For a normal distribution, enter the mean, a positive standard deviation, and two bounds. The normal mode supports -inf and inf for open-ended intervals and shades the requested interval on a bell curve.

Independent-Event Probability Formulas

If A and B are independent, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). The complement of A is P(A′) = 1 − P(A), and similarly for B. The probability of at least one event is P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A)P(B).

  • Both occur: P(A ∩ B) = ab
  • At least one: P(A ∪ B) = a + b − ab
  • Exactly one: P(A Δ B) = a(1 − b) + (1 − a)b
  • Neither: P(A′ ∩ B′) = (1 − a)(1 − b)
  • Only A: a(1 − b); only B: (1 − a)b

These formulas depend on the independence assumption. For dependent events, P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B | A), which generally differs from the formula used here.

Worked Example: Two Events

Let P(A) = 0.5 and P(B) = 0.4. Then both occur with probability 0.5 × 0.4 = 0.2 (20%). At least one occurs with probability 0.5 + 0.4 − 0.2 = 0.7 (70%). Exactly one occurs with probability 0.5 + 0.4 − 2 × 0.2 = 0.5 (50%), and neither occurs with probability 0.5 × 0.6 = 0.3 (30%).

Repeated Independent Trials

When the same independent event with success probability p is repeated n times, the probability of success on every trial is pn. The chance of failure on every trial is (1 − p)n; the chance of at least one success is 1 − (1 − p)n. These are different questions and give different results.

For example, if A has success probability 0.6 repeated five times, the chance of five successes is 0.65 = 0.07776. If B has success probability 0.3 repeated three times, the chance of three successes is 0.33 = 0.027. The probability of both full-success sequences is 0.07776 × 0.027 = 0.00209952.

Normal Distribution Probability

A normal random variable X has a mean μ and a standard deviation σ. Convert a bound x to a standard score using z = (x − μ) / σ. The probability between the bounds L and R is P(L ≤ X ≤ R) = Φ((R − μ) / σ) − Φ((L − μ) / σ), where Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

For mean 0, standard deviation 1, and bounds −1 to 1, the probability is approximately 0.68268949 (68.268949%). The calculator also displays common central coverage intervals for a normal distribution. These are not confidence intervals for estimating a mean from a sample.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should probabilities be entered as percentages?

Enter probabilities on a 0–1 scale: for example, 0.25 for 25%. The result tables show both decimal values and percentages.

What if the solver says there are two solutions?

Some combinations, such as the probability of both events and the probability of either event, determine the two individual probabilities but do not reveal which belongs to A and which belongs to B. The tool displays both valid assignments.

What if the solver reports insufficient information?

Two entered values can be mathematically redundant. For example, P(A ∪ B) = 0.7 and P(neither) = 0.3 represent just one equation. A third independent relation may be necessary.

Does this calculator work for dependent events?

The two-event and repeated-event sections specifically assume independence. For dependent events, use conditional probabilities or a probability-tree model instead.

Can I calculate a one-sided normal probability?

Yes. Use -inf as the left bound to calculate P(X ≤ R), or inf as the right bound to calculate P(X ≥ L).
