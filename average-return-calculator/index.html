Average Return Calculator

An average return can mean different things depending on the information you have. If you know an account’s opening and closing balances plus the dates of contributions and withdrawals, a cash-flow-based annual return can account for when money entered or left the account. If you know investment returns for successive holding periods, you can compound them to find a cumulative return and its annualized equivalent.

Which calculation should you use?

Account cash flows: choose “Average Return Based on Cash Flow” when you have starting and ending account balances and a dated record of deposits or withdrawals. The annualized rate is influenced by the amounts and timing of those external cash flows, so it describes the investor’s money-weighted experience.

A series of investment returns: choose “Average and Cumulative Return” when you have consecutive period returns and each holding period’s length in years and months. It combines the returns by compounding rather than treating percentages as dollars. In an investment-performance context, chained period returns reflect time-weighted growth when the individual periods appropriately exclude external cash-flow effects.

Neither mode asks for an accounting project's average profit or book value. Those are inputs for a different measure sometimes called ARR.

How to enter balances and transactions

Enter the Starting Balance and its date, then the Ending Balance and its date. For each intervening transaction, select its activity (deposit or withdrawal), enter the amount, and supply the transaction date. The observed form displays eight transaction rows and a “Show More Input Fields” control that exposes additional rows; use only the rows you actually need. Record deposits as additions of outside money and withdrawals as money leaving the account, not as market gains or losses.

Dates matter: an early contribution has more time exposed to investment returns than the same contribution added near the end. Use consistent currency units and account values measured at the stated dates. Where possible, base balances on records that include reinvested distributions and reflect the fee basis you intend to measure.

What a cash-flow-based return means

The concept is a money-weighted return, commonly calculated as an internal rate of return (IRR) from dated cash flows. Under the usual investor sign convention, the initial investment and later deposits are negative cash flows (money paid into the account); withdrawals and the terminal account value are positive. A rate is sought that discounts those dated amounts to a net present value of zero.

Dated-cash-flow equation: 0 = Σ [CFᵢ / (1 + r)^(dᵢ / 365)], where CFᵢ is a signed cash flow on date i, dᵢ is the number of days from the first date, and r is an annual rate. This is a standard ACT/365 illustrative convention; the exact day-count convention and numerical algorithm used by the live calculator have not been verified.

Crucially, a deposit increases the account balance but is not itself investment profit. The equation compares dollars invested at different times; it does not simply divide the difference between ending and starting balances by the initial balance.

Worked example: a contribution in the middle

Suppose an account starts with $10,000 on January 1, 2025. You deposit another $5,000 on January 1, 2026, and its value reaches $17,600 on January 1, 2027. Assume no other deposits, withdrawals, fees, or taxes for this illustration. The two intervals are each 365 days.

At a 10% annual return, $10,000 grows to $11,000 in the first year. After the $5,000 deposit, $16,000 grows to $17,600 in the second year. Check: $10,000 × 1.10² + $5,000 × 1.10 = $17,600. Therefore, the example’s annual money-weighted return is 10.00%. The investment gain is $17,600 − $10,000 − $5,000 = $2,600, not $7,600.

Why the date of a deposit or withdrawal changes the result

A large deposit just before a market gain can raise an investor’s money-weighted return, whereas a large deposit just before a market decline can lower it, even when the underlying portfolio’s sequence of percentage returns is unchanged. This is why money-weighted performance is useful for evaluating an individual account’s experience, but time-weighted return is often preferable for comparing an investment manager to a benchmark.

An IRR calculation is not guaranteed to have a single sensible solution. Complex cash-flow patterns with repeated changes of sign can produce multiple roots or no acceptable root. A live tool may also limit acceptable dates, entries, or precision. Treat its displayed result as an estimate and check it against the transaction history.

Cumulative and annualized returns for successive holding periods

How to enter period returns and holding lengths

For each successive investment period, enter its Return as a percentage and its holding length using the Years and Months columns. For example, enter −10 for a 10% loss; do not enter −0.10 if the field expects a percentage. The visible form starts with eight rows, and “Show More Input Fields” exposes additional period rows. Use the same definition of return across all periods (for example, total return with distributions reinvested, on the same before- or after-fee basis).

Each period return must represent a distinct nonoverlapping interval, in chronological order. Do not enter two overlapping returns and compound them as if they occurred one after the other. If periods have different lengths, annualization uses the combined elapsed years, not simply the count of rows.

The compounded-growth formulas

For n consecutive holding periods, let rᵢ be each return expressed as a decimal and let T be the total duration in years. The growth factor is G = ∏(1 + rᵢ). Cumulative return = G − 1. Annualized compound return = G^(1/T) − 1, provided T > 0 and G is nonnegative. Multiply the final decimal by 100 to display a percentage.

When a holding length is given as whole years and months, an illustrative conversion is T = Σ (yearsᵢ + monthsᵢ/12). This is not the same as exact-date annualization, and rounding partial months or assuming every year is identical can cause small differences. The exact live implementation’s conversion and rounding conventions remain unverified.

For a single investment with no intermediate external cash flows, the same compound idea yields CAGR = (ending value / starting value)^(1/T) − 1. This balance-ratio shortcut is generally unsuitable when there were intermediate deposits or withdrawals.

The total growth factor is 1.20 × 0.90 × 1.08 = 1.1664, so the cumulative return is 16.64%. Total holding time is 1 + 0.5 + 1.5 = 3 years. Annualized return = 1.1664^(1/3) − 1 ≈ 5.2646% per year. Reading cumulative and average annual figures

Cumulative return answers how far the investment grew or shrank over the whole specified span. Annualized compounded return puts different investment horizons on a comparable per-year scale. A 16.64% cumulative gain across three years is not a 16.64% annual gain, nor is dividing 16.64% by 3 an exact annualization. The 5.26% figure is a compounded equivalent that respects the growth path.

A −100% period return makes the investment’s growth factor zero: the position is entirely lost and later percentage returns on that same extinguished position cannot restore its value without new capital. For an ordinary unleveraged holding, a quoted return below −100% is not a valid simple holding-period return; leveraged or liability-bearing arrangements require special treatment.

Arithmetic average, geometric average, money-weighted return, and ARR

“Average return” is ambiguous in finance. Specify the measurement method whenever you report a figure, especially if the investment saw substantial volatility or cash was added and removed. These measures are related but not interchangeable.

For the worked return sequence +20%, −10%, and +8%, the simple arithmetic mean is (20 − 10 + 8)/3 = 6.00% per observation, whereas the compounded annual rate across the specified three years is about 5.26%. These intervals are unequal in length, making the simple per-row average especially poor as a per-year performance figure. The arithmetic mean would describe an annual average only if the input observations were each one year long.

Consider the simpler equal-year case of +50% and −50%. Their arithmetic average is 0%, but the compounded wealth factor is 1.50 × 0.50 = 0.75: an actual cumulative loss of 25% and a two-year geometric average of approximately −13.40% per year. Percentage gains and losses are asymmetric because losses leave a smaller base for future growth.

Accounting rate of return (ARR) is a separate capital-budgeting metric. A typical expression is ARR = average annual accounting profit / average investment, though organizations may vary their denominator policy. It relies on accounting earnings rather than discounted dated cash flows, so it does not incorporate the time value of money and should not be substituted for the calculator’s two investment-return methods.

Accuracy, assumptions, and common mistakes

Cash flows versus performance: adding funds can increase ending value even when investments perform poorly. Separately identify each contribution and withdrawal; do not count the deposited principal as profit.

Periods versus annualized years: a quarterly, monthly, or multi-year return is not automatically a yearly rate. Use the period length to annualize with compounding. An arithmetic mean of unevenly spaced periods answers a different and usually less useful question.

Total return versus price change: distributions such as dividends and interest can be part of investment return. Compare consistent total-return definitions and recognize whether distributions were paid out or reinvested; do not double-count a distribution both as investment return and external deposit.

Fees, taxes, and inflation: a gross nominal return is not the investor’s after-fee, after-tax, inflation-adjusted purchasing-power return. The result reflects the account values and return inputs you supply; deductions not represented in your data will not be modeled independently.

Unknown algorithm and precision: the competitor’s static page identifies the two forms, but its interactive outputs, defaults, date-day-count basis, additional-row limits in practice, handling of multiple IRR roots, and validation messages were not independently tested here. Financial context: historical averages are descriptive statistics, not forecasts or guarantees. Compare investments over matching horizons, with matching fee and return definitions, and consider volatility and risk as well as average performance.
