What Is Inflation?

Inflation is a broad increase in the prices people pay for goods and services over time. As the overall price level rises, each dollar buys fewer goods and services. Deflation is the opposite: a broad fall in prices, often accompanied by greater purchasing power per dollar. The CPI is one commonly used measure of these changes, but individual spending patterns can differ significantly from the national average.

Why Prices Change

Inflation can stem from stronger demand relative to supply, rising production or transport costs, and expectations that influence wages and price setting. Supply disruptions and monetary policy may also affect the rate. Inflation is not necessarily uniform across categories: rent, food, energy, and medical costs may move differently from the overall index.

Inflation Formulas

Historical CPI-Based Adjustment

Adjusted amount = Starting amount × (Target CPI ÷ Starting CPI). The total percentage change in prices is (Target CPI ÷ Starting CPI − 1) × 100%. The ratio adjusts for the measured change in the overall price level; it is not a forecast or an investment return.

Worked example: January 2016 had a CPI-U of 236.916 and January 2017 had a CPI-U of 242.839. A $100 amount from January 2016 is equivalent to approximately $102.50 in January 2017, reflecting around 2.50% inflation. Both CPI observations are historical, not estimated.

Forward and Backward Flat-Rate Inflation

For a fixed annual inflation rate r (as a decimal), starting amount P, and number of years n, forward inflation uses Future = P × (1 + r)n. Backward inflation uses Past = P ÷ (1 + r)n.

For example, at 3% inflation for 10 years, $100 grows to about $134.39 in future prices. Conversely, a $100 current price corresponds to about $74.41 ten years earlier under the same assumption. A constant rate is a modeling assumption, not a prediction that real inflation will remain unchanged.

How to Interpret the Results

An adjusted value shows the amount of money required in the comparison period to buy an equivalent average basket of goods and services. A positive percentage change means the CPI rose between the selected periods; a negative change means the CPI fell. The chart tracks the adjusted purchasing-power equivalent across the selected span. Annual averages and monthly indices represent different measurement periods and can generate slightly different results.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does the historical calculator support dates before 1913?

No. The U.S. CPI-U series used here begins in January 1913. This version includes published monthly observations through September 2026. It does not forecast unpublished future months.

What does “Average” mean in the month selector?

Average represents the official calendar-year CPI average, not December or a single month's observation. It is available for completed years, including 2025. It is not offered for 2026 because full-year CPI information is not yet available.

Why can't I select October 2025?

The Bureau of Labor Statistics did not publish an October 2025 CPI-U observation because data collection was interrupted by a lapse in appropriations. A missing observation is not substituted or interpolated in this calculator.

Is CPI a perfect measure of my personal inflation?

No. CPI-U reflects a national urban-consumer market basket. Your own cost of living may rise or fall at a different rate depending on where you live and what you purchase.

Can the flat-rate calculators predict inflation?

They provide scenarios at rates you choose. They do not use a forecast model or guarantee future price movements.
