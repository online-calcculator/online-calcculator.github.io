Age Calculator

An age calculator finds the time between a date of birth and a date you choose. Calendar age is usually reported as completed years, followed by full months and remaining days. You can also express the same date interval in weeks or total days. These are different ways of describing one period, not different ages.

How Calendar Age Is Calculated

First count complete birthdays. A quick starting rule is: completed years = reference year − birth year, subtracting 1 if the birthday has not yet occurred in the reference year. On the birthday itself, the new full year has been completed.

Next count complete calendar months after the latest completed birthday, then the remaining calendar days. For the example, May 15, 2000 → May 15, 2026 is 26 full years; May 15 → August 15 is 3 full months; August 15 → August 20 is 5 days. The result is 26 years, 3 months, 5 days.

This method follows actual calendar anniversaries. Dividing total days by 365 cannot give the same exact calendar age because years and months have unequal lengths.

Why Month-End Dates Can Produce Different Answers

A calendar month is not a fixed number of days. For dates in the middle of a month, counting is straightforward: February 20 to March 20 is one calendar month. When the first or last date falls near a month end, more than one convention can be reasonable.

For instance, February 28, 2022 to March 31, 2022 is 31 elapsed days. An anniversary-based method counts February 28 to March 28 as one month, then three days to March 31: 1 month, 3 days. A month-end-alignment method can instead call the entire interval one month because both dates are the last day of their months. The two labels use different conventions; neither changes the 31-day total.

Similar questions arise around the 29th, 30th, and 31st, particularly when the following month has fewer days. For an official deadline or contractual age requirement, follow the rule specified by the relevant institution.

What happens if the birth date is February 29?

A leap year adds February 29. Under the Gregorian calendar, a year divisible by 4 is generally a leap year, except a century year must also be divisible by 400. Thus 2000 was a leap year, but 2100 will not be.

A person born on February 29 has no matching calendar date in most years. Some age-counting rules use February 28 as the non-leap-year anniversary; others use March 1. This may affect exactly when completed age advances, and a legal or administrative rule can differ from the convention used in a date calculator. Confirm the applicable rule when that single day matters.

Calendar Age and Elapsed Time Are Not Identical Measures

Completed years, months, and days use calendar boundaries. Total elapsed days are found by subtracting one date from the other, excluding the starting date and including each subsequent day through the reference date. For the example, the total is 9,593 days, or 1,370 complete weeks and 3 days.

Hours, minutes, and seconds shown by a date-only calculator are normally unit conversions: days × 24, × 1,440, and × 86,400. They assume each calendar day represents 24 hours. Without birth times and time zones, such totals should not be interpreted as precise elapsed clock time across daylight saving changes.

Age-Counting Conventions Can Differ

The common completed-years system starts at age 0 on the date of birth and increases by one on each birthday. Someone who has lived 3 years and 11 months is still 3 years old until the next birthday. The expression “in the fourth year of life” describes that same person without saying they have already turned 4.

Other traditions have counted years differently. For example, one traditional Chinese counting practice assigns age 1 at birth and advances the count at the lunar new year, rather than on each person's birthday. This is a cultural illustration, not a claim that everyone in China uses that system today. Use the convention required by the form or context in question.
