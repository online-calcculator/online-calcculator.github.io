    How to Use the Mutual Fund Calculator

    Enter the amount you plan to invest initially, plus any monthly or annual contributions. Specify the fund's assumed annual return, holding period (years and additional months), front-end sales charge, possible deferred sales charge, and annual operating expense ratio. Select Calculate to refresh the estimate and chart without leaving the page.

    The default example uses a $20,000 initial purchase, $1,000 contributed at the end of each month for five years, a 5% gross annual return, a 2% sales charge, and 0.5% annual operating expenses. Select Clear to erase the inputs or save in the results bar to print or save the page as a PDF.

    How the Mutual Fund Calculation Works

    Ending fund value

    This model uses monthly compounding and assumes a constant annual return. The illustrative annual return after expenses is the stated annual return minus the annual operating expense ratio. The equivalent monthly growth factor is (1 + net annual rate)1/12. The front-end sales charge reduces the initial investment and every subsequent purchase.

    Each month, the existing balance grows at the net monthly rate. Then the monthly contribution, net of its sales charge, is invested. At the end of each completed year, any annual contribution is added in the same way. The deferred sales charge, if entered, is deducted from the final pre-redemption balance, using the lesser of contributed principal and the pre-redemption balance as its basis.

    Net return, charges, and IRR

    Total principal equals the initial investment plus all contributions. Net return equals ending value minus total principal. The sales charge is the given percentage of total principal. Operating expenses shown in dollars are a monthly approximation based on the beginning-of-month balance; the expense ratio is already included in the net growth assumption and is not subtracted again. Consequently, expense-dollar totals can vary modestly from another fund calculator that accrues fees daily or uses a different base.

    Net IRR is the effective annual internal rate of return for the investor's cash flows: the initial investment at month zero, later contributions at the ends of their respective months, and the ending value as a final inflow. A monthly discount rate is found by solving the net-present-value equation, and it is annualized as (1 + monthly IRR)12 − 1. This helps compare scenarios with different contribution schedules.

    Worked Example

    Invest $20,000, add $1,000 monthly for 60 months, assume a 5% annual return before 0.5% annual operating expenses, and apply a 2% front-end load. Total principal is $80,000 and the up-front charges across all purchases total $1,600. Under the simplified monthly-growth assumptions, the projected ending value is about $90,077.09, the net gain is about $10,077.09, and the net annualized IRR is approximately 3.844%. The fee amount shown separately is an estimate and depends on the expense-accrual convention.

    Frequently Asked Questions

    Does this calculator predict an actual mutual fund's performance?

    No. Real market returns fluctuate, and fund fees and share-class rules vary. This tool models a constant assumed return and simplified fees; it does not predict security prices, distributions, taxes, or future fund performance.

    What is a sales charge?

    A front-end sales charge is a percentage withheld from a fund purchase before it is invested. A deferred sales charge is collected when the investment is redeemed. Some funds have charges that decline with the holding period, so check the fund's prospectus.

    Why is the IRR different from the rate of return entered?

    The entered rate is the annual growth assumption before operating expenses. The investor's IRR also reflects sales charges, a potential redemption charge, and the timing of contributions.

    Are monthly and annual contributions made at the beginning or end of a period?

    They are modeled at the end of each month or completed year, after that period's investment growth. Different timing assumptions can change the result.

    Where can I learn more about mutual fund fees?

    See Investor.gov's mutual fund education and the SEC's mutual fund fee calculator FAQ. The reference mutual fund calculator also explains loads, operating expenses, and IRR. The simplified model here is intended for educational comparison, not personalized investment advice.
