How to Use the Debt Consolidation Calculator

Enter the balance, monthly payment, and annual interest rate for each existing debt. The first three rows contain sample values. You can enter up to 20 debts by selecting Show more input fields. Leave unused rows blank.

Next, enter the consolidation loan amount, annual interest rate, term in years and additional months, and loan fees as either a percentage of the loan amount or a fixed dollar amount. Select Calculate to see the comparison above the form. Clear removes all entries, and Print / Save opens the browser’s print dialog, where you can save a PDF.

How the Calculations Work

Current debt payoff and APR

Each active debt accrues monthly interest equal to balance × annual rate ÷ 1,200. The calculator applies each debt’s entered monthly payment, then redirects money freed by paid-off debts to the remaining debt with the highest interest rate. The total monthly payment budget is held constant until the final smaller payment. This method estimates total payments, interest, and the time to repay all debts, assuming no new borrowing or changes to interest rates.

The combined existing-debt APR is the annualized monthly internal rate of return (IRR): the rate at which the present value of simulated future payments equals the total original debt balance. It reflects the actual payment schedule rather than simply averaging the individual rates.

New consolidation loan payment

The fixed monthly payment uses the standard amortization formula: payment = P × r ÷ [1 − (1 + r)−n], where P is the stated loan principal, r is the annual interest rate divided by 1,200, and n is the term in months. If the interest rate is zero, payment equals P divided by n.

The loan fee is the entered fixed dollar amount or loan amount multiplied by the fee percentage. Because this comparison assumes the fee is deducted from the new loan proceeds, usable proceeds = loan amount − loan fee. The adjusted loan APR is calculated from the same loan payments but uses the usable proceeds as the amount received. The upfront cash flow is usable proceeds minus the current debt balance.

Total cost and savings comparison

Estimated consolidation net outlay equals total new loan payments minus the upfront cash flow. A negative cash flow is money you must contribute to close the old debts; positive cash flow is unused proceeds you receive. Estimated savings = total existing debt payments − consolidation net outlay. The result also shows the monthly payment change and payoff duration, which may differ from total cost savings.

Worked Example

Suppose your debts are $10,000 at 17.99% with a $260 monthly payment, $7,500 at 19.99% with a $190 payment, and $6,500 at 18.99% with a $180 payment. The combined starting balance is $24,000 and monthly payments total $630. Under the higher-rate-first payment reallocation assumption, payoff takes about 59 months, with roughly $12,963 in interest.

A $25,000 consolidation loan at 10.99% for 60 months has a payment of about $543.44. A 5% fee is $1,250, leaving $23,750 of usable proceeds, so an additional $250 is needed to repay all existing balances. The fee-adjusted loan APR is about 13.25%. Compare the complete cash outlays rather than judging the loan by interest rate alone.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a lower monthly payment always mean the loan is cheaper?

No. A lower payment can result from a longer repayment period and can increase total interest. Compare the full repayment totals, term, fees, and cash contribution.

Why is the fee-adjusted APR higher than the stated interest rate?

An origination fee reduces the funds you actually receive while leaving the scheduled loan payments unchanged. This increases the effective borrowing cost and the resulting APR.

What if the consolidation loan is smaller than my current balances?

The calculator shows the additional cash required after any loan fee is deducted. That shortfall is included in the estimated net outlay so the comparison does not overlook your initial contribution.

Are changing minimum payments, promotional rates, and new spending included?

No. This model assumes fixed annual rates, constant initial payment amounts, monthly compounding, no new purchases, and a redirected payment budget as debts are paid off. Actual lender disclosures, variable rates, penalty fees, and borrower behavior can change results.
