How to Use the Average Calculator

Enter the numbers in one list, with a comma between entries, for example, 4, 8, 12, 16. Include each observation you want counted. If a number occurs twice, enter it twice. Run the calculation to get the arithmetic mean; the example list has an average of 10.

The reference tool shows a single comma-separated entry field rather than separate arithmetic, weighted, or geometric-mean modes. For decimals or negative values, use valid numeric notation; the exact live input validation of this tool has not been independently tested. A list with no valid numbers does not have a defined mean.

What Is the Arithmetic Mean?

The arithmetic mean is the sum of all observations divided by the number of observations. It includes every value, so unusually high or low numbers can change the result. A mean does not have to be one of the values in the original list.

Average (arithmetic mean) = (x₁ + x₂ + ⋯ + xₙ) ÷ n = sum ÷ count

Here, x₁ through xₙ are the individual numbers and n is their count, with n greater than zero. Use the same unit for all values; an average of lengths, for instance, remains a length.

Worked Example: Find the Average of Four Numbers

Suppose the number of deliveries made on four days is 4, 8, 12, and 16.

1. Add the values: 4 + 8 + 12 + 16 = 40 deliveries.

2. Count the values: n = 4 days.

3. Divide the sum by the count: 40 ÷ 4 = 10 deliveries per day.

The result describes an even daily share of the total; it does not mean 10 deliveries occurred on every day.

Why the Average Formula Works

Think of the mean as balancing a total across equally many entries. For 2, 4, and 9, the sum is 15 and there are three entries. Sharing 15 equally gives 5, 5, and 5. This preserves the sum but replaces the uneven values with the common arithmetic mean.

How to Interpret the Result

A mean is useful when you want an equal-share summary, such as average daily sales, average distance, or average test score. But it can be pulled toward extreme values. For example, the list 10, 10, 10, 50 has a mean of 20 even though three of its four values are 10. Its median, the middle value after sorting, or the midpoint of two middle values, is 10.

A simple average gives each listed entry equal weight. If entries represent groups of different sizes, averaging their group averages directly can be wrong. For example, a group of 10 students with an average score of 70 and a group of 30 students with an average score of 90 have an overall average of (10 × 70 + 30 × 90) ÷ 40 = 85, not 80. The two group means alone cannot produce the combined mean without their group sizes.

Input Checks and Common Mistakes

Count observations, not commas or distinct values. Keep all values in compatible units. Do not remove zero simply because it is zero; exclude a value only when your data-collection rules justify doing so. Do not treat missing data as zero unless zero is the recorded measurement. If the list is empty, division by its count would require division by zero, so the mean is undefined.

The order of the entries does not change their mean. For reporting, round the final answer to a precision appropriate for your source data rather than assuming every decimal digit is meaningful.
