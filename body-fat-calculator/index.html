Body Fat Calculator

Body fat percentage is the estimated share of your total body weight made up of fat. This calculator uses body measurements to estimate that percentage with a traditional U.S. Navy circumference equation and can also show a second, BMI-based estimate. It is useful for understanding body composition and following broad trends, but neither number is a direct measurement or a medical diagnosis.

How to use the body fat calculator

Choose U.S. or metric units in the tabs above the calculator. Select Other Units to open the length, temperature, area, volume, and weight converter above the calculator; the individual height, circumference, and weight dropdowns also let you use other units directly. Enter age, weight, standing height, neck circumference, and waist/abdomen circumference. The female equation also uses hip circumference. The age and weight fields have different roles: weight converts the estimated percentage into fat and lean mass; age is used in the BMI-based estimate and the calculator’s age-reference display, not in the original Navy circumference equation.

Values are converted internally before calculation, so changing a unit keeps the same physical measurement. Measure circumferences consistently to about ¼ inch (0.5 cm) as the reference page advises. The Navy equation uses metric values internally; its result is independent of the display units you select.

After calculating, read the Navy estimate first, then compare the secondary BMI estimate and the body-fat category. Any age-specific “ideal” value or suggested fat-to-lose amount is a reference-based projection, not a personalized instruction to lose weight. The published comparator includes a save-calculation control and a results graphic; whether your deployed site has these functions must be verified in the working tool.

What the result means

Navy body fat percentage and category

A result such as 16.4% means the equation estimates about 16.4% of total body mass is fat. It does not mean 16.4% was directly measured. The accompanying category is a label from a historical fitness reference chart; labels such as “Fitness,” “Average,” and “Obese” are not diagnoses or guarantees of good or poor health.

Fat mass and lean body mass

Estimated fat mass = total body weight × (body fat percentage ÷ 100). Estimated lean body mass = total body weight − estimated fat mass. “Lean body mass” here includes muscle, organs, bone, water, and other nonfat tissue; it is not the same as skeletal muscle mass. For a hypothetical 82 kg adult with an estimated 16.4% body fat: fat mass ≈ 82 × 0.16436 = 13.5 kg, while lean mass ≈ 68.5 kg. These two values add back to about 82 kg; slight differences reflect rounding.

Age-reference and “fat to lose” outputs

The benchmark tool displays an age-linked reference called “Ideal Body Fat for Given Age (Jackson & Pollock)” and a derived “Body Fat to Lose to Reach Ideal.” The displayed age value is not an evidence-based personal target. A fat-to-lose calculation also assumes that all the mass lost is fat and lean mass stays unchanged, an assumption that may not hold in reality. For any hypothetical target p expressed as a decimal, target weight = current estimated lean mass ÷ (1 − p), and modeled weight change = current weight − target weight. This algebra illustrates an assumption, not a recommendation.

Why the BMI estimate can disagree

The BMI-based equation uses weight, height, age, and the equation’s sex term, but no tape circumferences. The Navy method uses height and circumferences but not age or weight for its percentage calculation. Different inputs and statistical models can produce noticeably different answers in the same person. Neither should be treated as a definitive tie-breaker.

How to take the body measurements

Use a flexible, nonstretch tape; stand relaxed, keep the tape snug without indenting skin, and measure after a normal exhale. For trend comparisons, repeat the same method and measurement landmarks each time. If practical, take two readings and remeasure if they disagree substantially.

Neck: Measure immediately below the larynx (voice box), with the tape angled slightly downward toward the front. Keep the neck relaxed; do not push the head forward or flare the neck.

Male abdomen: Measure horizontally at the level of the navel, without pulling the stomach inward. This is not necessarily the same location as a clinical waist measurement above the hip bones.

Female waist and hip: Find the smallest horizontal waist circumference for the historical Navy equation, then measure the hips around the largest circumference of the buttocks. Keep the tape level for both.

Tape placement matters because the equations respond to differences between circumference measurements. A change caused by a different waist landmark, breathing pattern, or tape tension may look like a change in body fat even when actual fat mass is unchanged.

How the U.S. Navy formula works

Hodgdon and Beckett’s 1984 circumference approach estimates body density from body shape, then converts density into body-fat percentage. The metric and U.S.-customary versions shown by the benchmark are separate coefficient forms, not one formula into which centimeters and inches can be swapped freely. All logarithms below are base 10, written log₁₀. Circumferences must be positive, and the logarithm’s interior must be greater than zero.

Male equation

Metric version (all lengths in centimeters): D = 1.0324 − 0.19077 × log₁₀(W − N) + 0.15456 × log₁₀(H); body fat % = 495 ÷ D − 450. Here D is estimated body density, W is abdomen circumference at the navel, N is neck circumference, and H is standing height. In particular, W must be greater than N.

U.S.-customary version (all lengths in inches): body fat % = 86.010 × log₁₀(W − N) − 70.041 × log₁₀(H) + 36.76. The inch-based version and density-based metric version may differ slightly because their coefficients are rounded independently.

Worked male example, metric: Suppose height = 178 cm, neck = 38 cm, abdomen = 85 cm, age = 35 years, and weight = 82 kg. The circumference difference is 85 − 38 = 47 cm. D = 1.0324 − 0.19077 log₁₀(47) + 0.15456 log₁₀(178) ≈ 1.06124; 495 ÷ 1.06124 − 450 ≈ 16.44% body fat. Age and weight do not enter this Navy percentage, but weight determines the fat mass shown in the result.

Female equation

Metric version (all lengths in centimeters): D = 1.29579 − 0.35004 × log₁₀(W + P − N) + 0.22100 × log₁₀(H); body fat % = 495 ÷ D − 450. Here W is the narrowest-waist measurement, P is the hip circumference at its widest point, N is neck circumference, and H is standing height. W + P − N must be positive.

U.S.-customary version (all lengths in inches): body fat % = 163.205 × log₁₀(W + P − N) − 97.684 × log₁₀(H) − 78.387.

Worked female example, metric: Suppose height = 165 cm, neck = 33 cm, waist = 75 cm, and hips = 100 cm. The combined circumference term is 75 + 100 − 33 = 142 cm. D = 1.29579 − 0.35004 log₁₀(142) + 0.22100 log₁₀(165) ≈ 1.03247; 495 ÷ 1.03247 − 450 ≈ 29.43%. At 68 kg, estimated fat mass is approximately 20.0 kg and lean mass is about 48.0 kg.

The alternative BMI-based body fat method

Body mass index (BMI) = weight in kilograms ÷ [height in meters]². It describes weight relative to height, not the percentage of fat itself. The benchmark also uses prediction equations published by Deurenberg and colleagues, who modeled estimated body-fat percentage from BMI, age, and sex in a particular research population.

Adults: For the male equation, body fat % = 1.20 × BMI + 0.23 × age − 16.2. For the female equation, body fat % = 1.20 × BMI + 0.23 × age − 5.4. Age is in years. The shared form is 1.20 × BMI + 0.23 × age − 10.8 × S − 5.4, where S = 1 for male and 0 for female in the original model.

Child research equations (presented for completeness, not as a pediatric diagnosis): Boys: body fat % = 1.51 × BMI − 0.70 × age − 2.2. Girls: body fat % = 1.51 × BMI − 0.70 × age + 1.4. The original paper distinguished younger children from adults; the child model explained substantially less of the observed variation. Children and teens should be assessed using age-appropriate growth information and professional context rather than the adult category bands below.

Working adult comparison: The 35-year-old man weighing 82 kg at 1.78 m has BMI = 82 ÷ 1.78² ≈ 25.88. The BMI body-fat prediction is 1.20 × 25.88 + 0.23 × 35 − 16.2 ≈ 22.9%. His Navy example gave about 16.4%. The 6.5-percentage-point gap shows why results from different prediction equations must not be treated as interchangeable measurements.

Body fat categories: use the labels as context, not diagnosis

The historic American Council on Exercise (ACE) reference commonly reproduced by fitness calculators groups adult estimates into descriptive bands. The upper band is called “Obese” in that chart, but these cutoffs are expert-opinion conventions, not clinically validated universal thresholds. Body composition, age, reproductive physiology, ethnicity, and measurement error affect interpretation; a personal health evaluation also considers waist distribution, laboratory findings, medical history, and other risk factors.

What body fat can and cannot, tell you about health

Adipose tissue stores energy, helps cushion and insulate the body, and releases signaling substances involved in metabolism. Essential fat supports normal function; stored fat exists beneath the skin (subcutaneous fat) and around internal abdominal organs (visceral fat). The location of fat can matter as much as its total amount. Higher visceral or central fat accumulation is associated with greater cardiometabolic risk, including insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

Body weight alone does not reveal tissue composition. Two people can have the same weight or BMI but different amounts of muscle and fat. Changes over the life course, hormonal status, activity, illness, medication, and genetics may affect fat distribution. Conversely, exceptionally low fat estimates should not be assumed safer; low energy availability and inadequate fat reserves can be harmful.

The BMI estimate and tape method do not directly measure visceral fat. For a fuller picture, healthcare professionals may combine repeated measurements with waist-based risk assessment, blood pressure, laboratory results, and clinical history. Do not equate a calculator’s “obese” category with a diagnosis, and do not use it to decide on aggressive weight loss.

Other ways to assess body composition

Tape-based Navy estimate: inexpensive, accessible, and repeatable when landmarks are used consistently, but sensitive to body proportions and measurement technique. BMI-based estimate: requires only basic measurements and age, but is indirect and cannot distinguish muscle from fat.

Bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA): estimates composition using electrical impedance, but hydration and device algorithms can shift the reading. Skinfold calipers: estimate subcutaneous fat through several measured sites, with substantial dependence on tester technique and population-specific equations.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA): estimates fat, lean soft tissue, and bone mineral compartments using specialized equipment; it is more informative but not perfect and has access, cost, and equipment limitations. Air-displacement plethysmography and underwater weighing estimate body density using different physical principles, but require controlled testing and assumptions about tissue densities. A sophisticated test is still an estimate with its own limitations.

Tips for interpreting changes over time

💡 Tip: Compare measurements taken under similar conditions, with the same tape and body landmarks. Record the measurement date and method, not just the rounded percentage. Look for a sustained pattern over several readings rather than responding to a single small change. A difference of one or two percentage points may reflect technique or model variability rather than real tissue change.

If measurements are physiologically impossible (for example, a nonpositive waist-minus-neck term), the method is undefined and no credible percentage should be shown. Very unusual body proportions, pregnancy, edema or major fluid changes, medical conditions affecting body composition, and use outside the derivation population warrant additional caution. Sex-specific historical equations may not appropriately represent every individual’s physiology.

If a result raises concerns about health, eating behavior, or an unusually low target, speak with a qualified healthcare professional. A calculator is most useful as one piece of context, not a prescription.
