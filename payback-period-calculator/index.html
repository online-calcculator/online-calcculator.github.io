How to Use the Payback Period Calculator

Choose Fixed Cash Flow when annual receipts follow a predictable growth or decline rate. Enter the initial investment, first year's cash flow, direction and percentage of change, number of years, and annual discount rate. Select Calculate to see the ordinary payback, discounted payback, annual return rate, and the year-by-year schedule directly above the form.

Choose Irregular Cash Flow Each Year when annual receipts vary. Enter the investment amount, annual discount rate, and the cash flows in chronological order. The first eight years appear initially; use Show More Input Fields to enter up to 100 years. A blank year between entered values counts as zero, and trailing blank years are excluded. Negative entries represent cash outflows.

Payback Period Formula

Payback period measures how long cumulative cash inflows take to recover an initial cash outlay. For equal positive annual cash flows, the basic formula is:

Payback period = Initial investment ÷ Annual cash flow.

For changing annual receipts, the calculator adds cash flows to the unrecovered balance each year. When that balance first reaches zero, it estimates the recovery point with fractional-year linear interpolation:

Payback = Full years before recovery + (Unrecovered balance at previous year-end ÷ Cash flow during recovery year).

Discounted Payback Period Formula

Discounted payback accounts for the time value of money. Each year's future cash flow is converted to present value before calculating the cumulative balance:

Present value of year t cash flow = CFt ÷ (1 + r)t, where r is the annual discount rate in decimal form.

The discounted payback period is found by interpolating within the first year that the cumulative discounted cash flow becomes nonnegative. If the balance never becomes nonnegative during the entered period, the result says the investment is not recovered within that time.

Cash Flow Return Rate (IRR)

The displayed cash-flow return rate is the estimated annual internal rate of return. It is the rate i satisfying 0 = −Initial investment + Σ[CFt ÷ (1 + i)t]. For conventional cash flows, the equation usually has one solution. With alternating positive and negative flows, there can be multiple solutions, in which case the tool indicates that the IRR is not unique.

Worked Examples

Fixed cash flow with annual growth

Invest $100,000 and receive $30,000 in year 1, increasing by 5% annually for five years. Receipts in years 2–5 are $31,500, $33,075, $34,728.75, and $36,465.19. Using year-by-year linear interpolation, the ordinary payback is approximately 3.156 years. With a 10% discount rate, discounted payback is approximately 3.921 years, and IRR is approximately 18.86% annually. A third-party reference may display slightly different fractions of a year because of a different intra-year interpolation convention; the full-year cash-flow schedule is the same.

Irregular yearly cash flow

Invest $100,000 and receive $5,000, $25,000, $35,000, $40,000, $30,000, and $10,000 in years 1–6. Ordinary payback is 3.875 years, while discounted payback at 10% is approximately 5.452 years. The annual IRR is approximately 10.96%.

Understanding the Schedule and Chart

The schedule starts at year 0 with a negative cash flow equal to the initial investment. Net Cash Flow accumulates the original yearly values, while Discounted Cash Flow shows each year's present value. Net Discounted Cash Flow accumulates those present values. Expand the optional chart to compare both running balances against the zero (break-even) line.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between simple and discounted payback?

Simple payback uses the amounts of cash received. Discounted payback adjusts future receipts for the annual discount rate, so it generally takes longer to recover the same initial investment when the rate is positive and cash flows are positive.

What if my investment never pays back?

The calculator reports that recovery is not reached within the entered projection period. You can extend the forecast to test later recovery, but do not assume the investment has an infinite economic life.

Can I use negative cash flows?

Yes, in the irregular schedule you can include additional outlays as negative amounts. This can create multiple break-even crossings and multiple possible IRRs. The displayed payback uses the first crossing, so examine the entire schedule and consider NPV as well.

Does a shorter payback always mean a better investment?

No. Payback does not capture the full value of all future cash flows, project size, taxes, financing structure, or investment risk. Compare it with net present value (NPV), IRR, and the investment's expected life.
