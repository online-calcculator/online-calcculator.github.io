How to Use the Circle Calculator

Enter one known measurement—radius, diameter, circumference, or area—and select Calculate. The calculator solves the other three values and shows each formula above the entry form. Editing a field clears the others, so an old number cannot conflict with a new one. Select Clear to start again, or use the save icon in the results bar to download your calculation as a text file.

Circle Formulas

A circle is the collection of points in a plane at a constant distance from its center. The four quantities are related by the mathematical constant pi, π ≈ 3.141592653589793.

QuantityFormulaMeaning
Radius (R)R = D/2 = C/(2π) = √(A/π)Center to edge
Diameter (D)D = 2RDistance across through the center
Circumference (C)C = 2πR = πDDistance around the circle
Area (A)A = πR² = πD²/4Space enclosed by the circle

Circle measurements at a glance

Worked Example: Area = 100

Suppose the area of a circle is 100 square units. The radius is R = √(100/π) ≈ 5.6418958354776 units. The diameter is D = 2R ≈ 11.283791670955 units, and the circumference is C = 2πR ≈ 35.44907701811 units. The calculator displays up to 14 significant digits, so values are rounded for presentation.

Units: If the radius is entered in centimeters, the diameter and circumference are also in centimeters, while the area is in square centimeters (cm²). This tool uses consistent user-defined units rather than converting between unit systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I find the radius from a circle's area?

Yes. Use the formula R = √(A/π), or enter the known area in the calculator.

What is the difference between radius and diameter?

The radius runs from the center to the perimeter. The diameter stretches through the center from one side to the other, and is twice the radius.

Can I enter a circumference instead of a radius?

Yes. Enter the circumference and the calculator determines the radius using R = C/(2π), along with the diameter and area.

Can I use a negative or zero measurement?

Negative dimensions are not valid. Zero is accepted mathematically as a degenerate circle with zero radius, diameter, circumference, and area.

Why does the output show so many decimal places?

Most circle calculations involve π, an irrational number. Results are shown to 14 significant digits to retain useful precision; measurements in real projects should be rounded to match the precision of the input data.
