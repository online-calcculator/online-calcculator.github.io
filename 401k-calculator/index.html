The accompanying guidance also explains how early distributions can reduce take-home proceeds and why paycheck-by-paycheck contribution timing can change the employer match. This is an educational projection, not a forecast of market performance, a tax return, or a determination of plan eligibility.

Choose the calculation that answers your question

  • Retirement savings projection: estimate the account balance at your expected retirement age and consider how long withdrawals might last.
  • Early withdrawal costs: estimate the amount remaining after assumed income taxes and a possible 10% additional federal tax.
  • Maximize employer match: find an employee contribution rate that captures matching contributions without exhausting your annual employee limit too early.

The three calculations answer different questions and must not be treated as interchangeable.

1. Estimate your 401(k) balance at retirement

Understand the savings inputs

Current age and expected retirement age establish the contribution horizon. Current annual salary is the pay used to estimate contributions; check whether your employer defines eligible compensation differently. Current 401(k) balance is the amount already invested, not the sum of past deposits. Contribution (% of salary) is your own payroll deferral, typically expressed as a percentage of eligible compensation.

Employer match is the employer's matching percentage of your contribution; employer match limit is the maximum portion of pay eligible for that match. For example, matching 50% of contributions on the first 6% of salary can supply up to 3% of salary if you contribute at least 6%. These fields are not an assumed flat employer contribution on your entire salary.

Expected salary increase sets annual pay growth. Expected annual return is a modeling assumption for account performance, not a promised yield; it should be interpreted consistently with fees. Expected inflation rate supports translation of future dollars into present-day purchasing power. Life expectancy helps frame how long retirement savings might need to support spending; it does not predict an individual's lifespan.

How an accumulation model works

For an illustrative annual model with deposits at each year-end, use Bₜ = Bₜ₋₁ × (1 + r) + Eₜ + Mₜ, where Bₜ is the year-end balance, Bₜ₋₁ is the preceding balance, r is the assumed annual net investment return, Eₜ is the employee deposit, and Mₜ is the employer deposit. Salary may grow as Sₜ = S₀ × (1 + g)^(t − 1), where S₀ is starting salary, g is annual salary growth, and t begins at 1. Apply contribution rates to that year's eligible salary and respect any applicable plan or statutory cap.

Actual calculators may apply contributions every paycheck, use monthly compounding, cap deposits at changing statutory limits, or use a different return convention. A year-end-deposit model generally differs from paycheck-by-paycheck results because earlier deposits can earn returns sooner. The numerical illustration below states its own convention; it is not presented as an exact observed output from the live calculator.

Worked example: a 30-year savings path

Assume someone age 35 already has $40,000 invested, earns $90,000, contributes 8% of salary, and receives a 50% match on the first 6% contributed. The resulting maximum match is 3% of salary. Assume a 2% raise each year, a constant 6% net nominal return, end-of-year deposits, no withdrawals, no binding contribution limits, and no additional fees. In year one, the employee deposits $7,200 and the employer deposits $2,700. The first year-end balance is $40,000 × 1.06 + $7,200 + $2,700 = $52,300.

Continue the recurrence for 30 years. At age 65 the illustrative balance is $1,202,942: the starting $40,000 plus approximately $292,090 of employee contributions, $109,534 of employer deposits, and $761,318 of modeled investment gains. Investment gains are a residual from the model, not a separate guaranteed deposit. With 2.5% annual inflation, this nominal result has purchasing power of approximately $573,494 in age-35 dollars: $1,202,942 ÷ 1.025³⁰.

Read the projection without mistaking it for spendable income

A projected account balance combines contributions, employer funding, and earnings; it is not automatically an after-tax retirement budget. For a traditional 401(k), withdrawals are generally taxable as ordinary income. Depending on their qualified status, Roth 401(k) withdrawals can receive different tax treatment. Fees, taxes, market volatility, sequence of returns, and different deposit timing can change realized results.

The comparison between nominal and inflation-adjusted values is especially important. If a balance grows while prices rise, the future dollar amount can look large without buying as much as today's dollars. To explore uncertainty, calculate at several plausible return assumptions rather than relying on one point estimate.

How withdrawals change a retirement projection

Once contributions stop, a simplified annual drawdown model is Bₜ = Bₜ₋₁ × (1 + r) − Wₜ, with Wₜ the withdrawal at the end of year t. If an account starts retirement at $500,000, earns a hypothetical 5% during the first year, and pays out $35,000 at year-end, the next balance is $500,000 × 1.05 − $35,000 = $490,000. This does not model tax withholding, market volatility, required distributions, or longevity risk. Life expectancy is a planning horizon, not a safe fixed end date.

IMPORTANT NOTE: A first-year withdrawal of 4% of an initial account is sometimes used as a starting planning heuristic. It is not a required distribution, a guaranteed sustainable rate, or a substitute for modeling actual expenses, investment returns, inflation, taxes, and the need to adjust spending.

2. Estimate the cost of an early 401(k) withdrawal

Inputs and a transparent net-proceeds example

The early-withdrawal tool asks for the withdrawal amount and assumed federal, state, and local/city income tax rates. It also asks about current employment, whether you were at least age 55 when you left employment, qualifying disability, and other possible penalty exceptions. These yes/no questions matter because a distribution that is permitted by the plan may or may not be exempt from the additional federal tax.

For a simple all-taxable traditional 401(k) distribution, a rough estimate is net cash = gross withdrawal − estimated federal income tax − estimated state/local tax − applicable additional 10% tax. Applying three rates directly to a withdrawal is only an approximation: actual tax depends on the full year's taxable income, marginal brackets, deductions, state law, withholding, and the character of the distribution.

Example: on a hypothetical $20,000 fully taxable distribution, assume incremental federal tax of 22%, state tax of 5%, no local tax, and a 10% additional tax with no exception. Estimated federal tax is $4,400, state tax is $1,000, additional tax is $2,000, and modeled net cash is $12,600. Withholding and final tax liability may differ.

When the additional 10% tax may not apply

Distributions from qualified retirement plans before age 59½ are generally subject to an additional 10% federal tax unless a statutory exception applies. Among the exceptions are distributions after separation from service in or after the calendar year you reach age 55 (for the relevant employer plan), qualifying disability, certain medical expenses, qualified domestic relations orders, and qualifying substantially equal periodic payments. Other specialized exceptions also exist. The plan's permission to withdraw and the IRS penalty exception are two separate tests.

IMPORTANT TAX NOTE: A hardship distribution is not automatically exempt from the 10% additional tax. A plan may permit hardship withdrawals for an immediate and heavy need, such as qualifying medical expenses, certain education costs, residence-related expenses, or certain disaster expenses; the plan sets its permitted conditions. Hardship distributions generally cannot be rolled over or repaid to replace the withdrawn savings. The mere fact of hardship does not itself waive income taxes or the additional tax.

💡 Tip: A plan loan is different from a distribution: where a plan permits loans, amounts repaid under the applicable rules are generally not currently taxed, but loan defaults or leaving employment can create tax consequences. Before withdrawing, review the plan document and consult an appropriate tax adviser for exception eligibility and the consequences of reducing future compounding.

3. Maximize employer matching contributions

Interpret a one-tier or two-tier match

The separate match-optimization section takes current age and annual salary plus Employer match 1 / Employer match 1 limit and Employer match 2 / Employer match 2 limit. It is designed to represent employer plans that can have two matching tiers. A tier may match a certain percentage of employee deposits up to a stated percentage of pay; the precise relationship between tiers depends on the plan formula.

With a single 50%-on-first-6% match and $90,000 salary, contributing 3% of pay generates $2,700 employee deposits and a $1,350 match. Contributing 6% produces $5,400 employee deposits and the full $2,700 match. Increasing the employee rate to 8% contributes $7,200 but ordinarily adds no more match under this formula. Additional saving may still be worthwhile, even after the match is maximized.

Why contributing too fast can reduce the match

Employers sometimes calculate matching deposits per paycheck and stop matching paychecks after your own employee deferrals hit the annual limit. For example, at $120,000 salary over 24 equal pay periods, each paycheck is $5,000. A 100% match on the first 5% of each paycheck could supply $250 per paycheck, or $6,000 in a full year. If the employee defers 30% ($1,500) every paycheck against the 2026 $24,500 regular deferral limit, the cap is reached on paycheck 17. With a simple per-paycheck match and no later true-up, only 17 × $250 = $4,250 of matching contributions would be credited; the last seven paychecks generate no match.

Distributing $24,500 across 24 paychecks instead requires about $1,020.83 per paycheck, or 20.4167% of the $120,000 annual salary, subject to payroll rounding. Under the same hypothetical rules, that could preserve all $6,000 of annual matching contributions. Some employers perform an annual true-up that restores missed matches; some plans have other restrictions. This is why the right 'window' of contribution rates depends on pay frequency, the employer formula, the tax year's deferral limit, and whether a true-up exists.

2026 IRS contribution limits and tax timing

These figures apply to most standard U.S. 401(k) plans for calendar year 2026, not to SIMPLE 401(k) plans. The employee elective-deferral limit is shared across applicable plans when a participant makes elective deferrals to multiple employers' plans. Employers may impose additional plan restrictions. Employer matching deposits are generally excluded from the employee elective-deferral ceiling but are included in the overall defined-contribution annual additions limit.

Traditional versus Roth 401(k)

A traditional pre-tax employee contribution generally reduces current federal taxable wages, while taxable withdrawals later are generally included in ordinary income. Designated Roth contributions are made after income tax and do not produce that same current exclusion; qualified Roth distributions can be tax-free if the five-taxable-year requirement and a qualifying age, disability, or death condition are met. An employer contribution may have its own pretax or designated Roth treatment if the plan supports the latter and the applicable requirements are met.

Traditional and Roth employee elective deferrals share one annual employee limit; you cannot contribute the full limit separately to each. An early nonqualified designated Roth withdrawal is generally allocated pro rata between contributions and earnings; its taxable earnings component may be subject to the additional early-distribution tax. Unlike Roth IRAs, a Roth 401(k) does not generally allow withdrawal of contributions first under an IRA-style ordering rule.

From 2024 onward, designated Roth accounts in qualified plans are no longer subject to lifetime required minimum distributions for the owner. Beneficiary rules differ. This is an important distinction from older explanations that say all Roth 401(k)s require distributions starting at 73.

What kind of retirement plan is a 401(k)?

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored defined contribution plan, so your retirement account value depends on deposits, investment performance, fees, and distributions. A traditional defined benefit pension instead promises a benefit determined under the pension's formula. A 401(k) balance can be moved in certain eligible rollovers when an employee changes jobs, but the plan's investment menu, payout options, vesting terms, and fees still matter.

Advantages, trade-offs, and investment options

Potential advantages include tax-favored retirement saving, relatively high contribution limits, and employer matching if provided. Employer matching is valuable but not literally unrestricted 'free money': vesting, eligibility, employer formulas, and payroll timing determine the amount you can retain. Many 401(k) plans also receive important creditor protections under applicable law, with exceptions depending on circumstances.

Trade-offs include restricted early access, plan-specific investment menus, administrative and investment expenses, and possible vesting rules on employer funding. Plans commonly offer mutual funds, index funds, target-date funds, or other portfolios mixing stocks and bonds. A target-date fund generally changes its allocation as its stated retirement date approaches, but does not remove market risk or guarantee an outcome.

Fee types can include plan administration charges, investment management/expense ratios, transaction charges, and optional service fees. Small annual percentage charges compound over decades. When comparing otherwise similar investments, distinguish gross expected returns from the net return after expenses that you use in a projection.

Vesting and eligibility

Your own employee elective deferrals are always vested, meaning they belong to you. Employer matching and some other employer contributions may vest immediately or according to a cliff or graded schedule. Cliff vesting grants ownership all at once after a stated period; graded vesting increases ownership step by step. If you leave before full vesting, the unvested employer-funded portion can be forfeited under the plan terms.

Your employer's summary plan description is the practical source for participation dates, eligible wages, matches, true-up policy, vesting, beneficiary designation, loans, and withdrawal rights. Generic waiting-period or vesting examples should not be assumed to describe your plan.

Taking distributions or moving your savings

Common retirement distribution choices

Depending on the plan and your circumstances, you may be able to keep assets invested, take an eligible lump sum, receive installments, elect a plan-offered annuity, or roll over eligible savings into another employer plan or an IRA. A lump sum can increase taxable income in the year received. Installments spread out payments but remain exposed to investment and longevity risks. An annuity option, where available, has contract-specific payments, survivor features, fees, and guarantees, so a simple investment projection is not a quote from an insurer.

An eligible direct rollover from a traditional 401(k) to an appropriately qualified traditional account generally avoids current taxation. Indirect rollovers can involve a 60-day deadline and withholding complications. A rollover from pretax money to a Roth account is generally a taxable conversion, not a tax-free transfer. Required minimum distributions and hardship distributions are not eligible rollovers. The general one-rollover-per-year limitation applies to certain IRA-to-IRA rollovers, not to eligible rollovers from employer plans.

Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

RMDs are minimum annual distributions from certain tax-deferred retirement accounts. For affected cohorts, the applicable starting age is generally 73; the age rises to 75 for people born in 1960 or later under SECURE 2.0. A participating employee who is not a 5% owner may, when the plan permits, delay RMDs from the current employer's qualified plan until retirement. The first required amount may generally be delayed until April 1 of the year after the starting year, though doing so can result in two taxable RMDs in that following year.

The basic calculation is RMD = prior December 31 account balance ÷ the applicable IRS distribution-period factor. The correct factor depends on age and certain beneficiary circumstances; use the IRS tables rather than dividing by a self-selected life expectancy. If a required amount is missed, the applicable excise tax is generally 25% of the shortfall, reduced to 10% if corrected within the specified period, and relief may be available for reasonable error. An older 50% figure is no longer the current standard.

Self-employed or solo 401(k)s

A self-employed person with qualifying self-employment income may establish an individual or solo 401(k) if plan eligibility rules are met. The owner may contribute in employee and employer capacities, subject to the separate and combined IRS limits and compensation rules. Certain solo/self-directed arrangements allow wider investments, but 'self-directed' does not mean prohibited transactions, valuation rules, fiduciary obligations, tax reporting, or investment risk disappear. Loans, where permitted, have distinct limits and repayment rules.

Assumptions, limitations, and sensible checks

No calculator knows future returns, inflation, future tax laws, future contribution limits, employer policy changes, retirement date, or future living expenses. Flat annual rates conceal market volatility and sequence risk; fixed life expectancy is an imperfect planning horizon. Cash flows modeled at the start versus end of a period change results. One stated annual return may be nominal or net of fees; do not subtract fees twice or ignore them.

For a useful sensitivity check, rerun the projection with a lower return, higher inflation, later or earlier retirement, a different salary path, and a different contribution percentage. Confirm whether a plan's matching dollars are immediately vested, whether the employer true-ups payroll matches, and whether your deferrals across all jobs share the IRS ceiling. For distributions, distinguish federal additional tax, ordinary income tax, state taxes, actual withholding, and plan permission.
