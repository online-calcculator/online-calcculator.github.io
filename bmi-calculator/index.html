BMI Calculator

Body mass index (BMI) compares weight with height. A BMI calculator gives you a numerical result and, for adults, a standard weight-status category. It is a quick screening starting point, not a direct body-fat measurement and not a diagnosis. For people ages 2 through 19, the same BMI number must be interpreted on age- and sex-specific growth charts.

    How to use the BMI calculator

    Enter your height and weight, selecting the units supplied by the tool. U.S. measurements typically use feet and inches for height and pounds for weight; metric measurements use centimeters or meters and kilograms. Where an age and sex field is available, complete it accurately, especially when evaluating a child or teen. Check whether the result is showing an adult category or a child BMI-for-age interpretation.

    Measure height without shoes and use a current weight, ideally from a reliable scale. The numerical result is usually rounded for display; a result near a category boundary can shift when more precise measurements are used. Use the actual BMI value, not only the rounded category label, for comparisons.

    What BMI tells you

    BMI is an index of mass relative to squared height, reported conventionally in kg/m². It helps identify weight-for-height patterns that can warrant further evaluation, and it is useful for population research. It cannot distinguish muscle from fat, show where fat is stored, or directly establish whether someone has a medical condition. A clinician may interpret BMI alongside blood pressure, metabolic tests, body composition, medical history, and other findings.

    BMI formulas and two worked examples

    Metric: kilograms and meters

    BMI = weight (kg) ÷ [height (m)]²

    Square the person's height in meters first, then divide kilograms by that squared value. For 70 kg and 1.72 m, height squared is 1.72 × 1.72 = 2.9584 m². BMI = 70 ÷ 2.9584 = 23.6614, or approximately 23.7 kg/m². Under standard adult classifications, that is within the healthy-weight range. This calculation does not estimate body-fat percentage.

    If height is entered in centimeters, the equivalent calculation is BMI = 10,000 × weight (kg) ÷ [height (cm)]². For 172 cm and 70 kg, this produces the same result.

    U.S. customary: pounds and inches

    BMI ≈ 703 × weight (lb) ÷ [height (in)]²

    Convert feet to inches before squaring. Someone who is 5 ft 6 in tall is 66 in tall. At 145 lb, BMI ≈ 703 × 145 ÷ 66² = 101,935 ÷ 4,356 = 23.4. The factor 703 is the conventional rounded unit conversion; very small differences can appear when a calculator uses more exact conversion constants.

    What is the healthy weight range for a given height?

    For adults: 18.5 × height (m)² ≤ weight (kg) < 25 × height (m)²

    At a height of 1.72 m, the standard BMI interval corresponds mathematically to about 54.7 kg up to, but not including, 74.0 kg (18.5 × 2.9584 = 54.73; 25 × 2.9584 = 73.96). These are reference-category boundaries, not a personalized goal weight or a treatment plan.

    Children and teens: use BMI-for-age percentiles

    For ages 2 through 19, a BMI number cannot be judged using adult cutoffs. Children's body composition changes with growth, so the CDC compares BMI with an age- and sex-specific reference population. A percentile indicates relative position on those growth charts: the 75th percentile means the measured BMI is at least as high as that of about 75% of peers of the same age and sex in the reference data; it is not a body-fat percentage.

    Severe obesity (subset)

    At least 120% of the 95th-percentile BMI, OR BMI ≥35 kg/m²

    Severe obesity is a subgroup of obesity, not a fifth disjoint range: it is defined as at least 120% of the BMI value at the 95th percentile for age and sex, or BMI at least 35 kg/m². A 95th percentile BMI of 24, for example, has a 120%-of-95th threshold of 28.8 kg/m². The age-specific chart and more precise age in months matter near boundaries.

    The CDC’s terminology is underweight, healthy weight, overweight, obesity, and severe obesity. Older materials may call the 85th–95th percentile “at risk of overweight,” but that is not the wording used in current CDC categories. A child’s growth pattern over time and clinical context matter more than one isolated percentile.

    Understanding BMI Prime

    BMI Prime scales an adult BMI relative to 25 kg/m², the upper boundary of the standard healthy-weight interval. It is a dimensionless ratio rather than a second independent body measurement.

    BMI Prime = BMI ÷ 25

    For the 70-kg, 1.72-m example, BMI Prime = 23.6614 ÷ 25 = 0.9465, usually reported as 0.95. A value of 1 means BMI 25; a value of 1.20 means BMI 30. Lower values do not automatically imply better health: BMI Prime below 0.74 corresponds to BMI below 18.5, the adult underweight threshold.

    Understanding the Ponderal Index

    The Ponderal Index (PI) also combines mass and height, but uses height cubed instead of squared in its metric form. It therefore changes differently with height than BMI. A PI should not be assumed to be a direct measurement of body composition or a universally superior substitute for BMI.

    Metric PI = weight (kg) ÷ [height (m)]³; units: kg/m³

    With 70 kg and a height of 1.72 m: PI = 70 ÷ (1.72 × 1.72 × 1.72) = 70 ÷ 5.088448 = approximately 13.76 kg/m³.

    A different customary-unit expression sometimes called the ponderal index is height (inches) ÷ cube root of weight (pounds). It has different physical units (in/lb^(1/3)) and the variables are arranged differently. Its numerical result should not be mixed with kg/m³ as though they were the same quantity. Interpretation depends on the exact convention used by the tool or study.

    What a high or low BMI may signal

    Higher BMI and health risk

    Across large adult populations, higher BMI categories are associated with increased likelihood of conditions such as elevated blood pressure, unhealthy blood lipids, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, sleep apnea, joint problems, and some cancers. Risk is not identical for every person at a given BMI. Fat distribution, physical activity, family history, and measured health indicators can materially change an individual's risk assessment.

    Lower BMI and health risk

    An unusually low adult BMI may prompt evaluation for inadequate intake, nutrient deficiencies, anemia, low bone density, illness, or unintended weight loss. Low BMI alone cannot identify the cause. In children, low BMI-for-age should be considered with the growth curve, nutrition history, and other developmental information rather than interpreted through adult numbers.

    Important limits and situations needing different interpretation

    Muscle and fat: Two people with identical BMI can have different amounts of body fat. An athlete with substantial lean mass may have a high BMI without correspondingly high fat mass; someone with a BMI in the standard healthy range can still have excess abdominal fat or metabolic risk.

    Age, sex, and body-fat distribution: BMI itself does not adjust the adult category thresholds for sex or age. However, the relationship between BMI and body composition varies across individuals and populations. Waist circumference, clinical history, and laboratory tests can provide complementary information.

    Asian populations: A WHO expert consultation retained the international BMI classifications but identified additional public-health action points, including 23 and 27.5 kg/m², because cardiometabolic risk may rise at lower BMI in some Asian populations. These are not a replacement one-size-fits-all diagnostic scale; the appropriate assessment depends on the population and clinical guidance.

    Pregnancy and special clinical settings: Ordinary nonpregnant adult BMI charts are not designed to interpret pregnancy-related weight change. Health professionals may use prepregnancy BMI for pregnancy weight-gain planning alongside other factors. Significant edema, amputation, or conditions that affect body proportions can also make routine BMI harder to interpret.

    Children: Use growth percentiles, not an adult weight-range chart. A child’s BMI percentile and growth trajectory should be interpreted by a qualified health professional when there is a concern.

    How to use a BMI result responsibly

    Treat the number as one piece of information. If it is outside the standard range or you have symptoms, unexpected weight changes, growth concerns, or questions about body composition, discuss it with a qualified healthcare professional. A BMI value cannot tell you whether to begin medication, follow a restrictive diet, or make other individualized treatment decisions.
