Choose the Right Calculation

  • Fix Length: Enter a starting principal, an assumed annual interest or return rate, a payout period in years (and any additional year/month field shown), and the payout frequency. The result is an estimated equal withdrawal per period that reduces the modeled balance to approximately zero at the end.
  • Fix Payment: Enter the starting principal, assumed annual rate, desired withdrawal per period, and the available payout frequency. The result is the estimated time until the balance is exhausted. If the proposed payment is too small to reduce the balance, there may be no finite payoff period.

Starting principal is the amount available when withdrawals begin, not necessarily the total amount originally paid into a contract. The rate describes assumed growth on the remaining balance; it is not necessarily a guaranteed net return. A payout frequency determines how many payments are made annually, so “$2,000 per month” and “$2,000 per year” are not equivalent.

How the Fixed-Length Payment Is Calculated

A fixed-length calculation spreads the starting balance across N equal payments while crediting growth to the unpaid balance. For equal end-of-period withdrawals, the present value of that payment stream equals the starting balance:

Payment = PV × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)^(−N)]

PV is the starting balance in dollars; i is the effective return per payment period as a decimal; N is the number of payments; and Payment is the dollars withdrawn each period. If there are m withdrawals per year for y years, N = m × y. For a stated effective annual return R, i = (1 + R)^(1/m) − 1. If i = 0, Payment = PV ÷ N.

This is an ordinary-annuity calculation: each withdrawal is assumed to occur at the end of its period, after that period’s return has been credited. A beginning-of-period withdrawal (annuity due), a nominal APR with separate compounding assumptions, or variable returns changes the result. Confirm the rate and timing convention before comparing the estimate with a contract.

Example: $240,000 Over 15 Years

Suppose a hypothetical balance of $240,000 earns a constant 5% effective annual return and supports monthly withdrawals for 15 years. There are 12 periods per year and 180 withdrawals. The monthly rate is (1.05)^(1/12) − 1 = approximately 0.00407412, or 0.4074%.

Monthly payment = $240,000 × 0.00407412 ÷ [1 − (1.00407412)^(−180)] = $1,884.05.

Over 180 modeled payments, the unrounded total is approximately $339,128.99. The difference from the initial $240,000, approximately $99,128.99, represents assumed growth credited during withdrawals, not extra deposits or a guaranteed investment profit. Displayed payments rounded to cents may cause a few cents of final-balance adjustment.

Reading the Balance Schedule

A schedule connects each year’s beginning balance, accumulated interest/return, withdrawals, and remaining ending balance. With end-of-month payments, each month follows: new balance = previous balance × (1 + i) − payment. The year’s interest/return is the sum of its 12 monthly credits; it falls over time as the remaining balance shrinks.

How Long Will a Fixed Payment Last?

Instead of choosing a duration, choose a payment. When i is positive and the periodic payment C exceeds the first period’s interest PV × i, the theoretical number of level payments before exhaustion is:

N = −ln(1 − PV × i ÷ C) ÷ ln(1 + i)

Here ln means natural logarithm. If the result is not a whole number, it represents an equivalent number of full payments; in practice, the last withdrawal can be smaller. If i = 0, N = PV ÷ C. If C ≤ PV × i with a strictly positive constant return, the balance will never be depleted under this simplified model. Negative or changing rates require a different projection.

Example: With the same $240,000 balance and 5% effective annual return, a requested $2,000 monthly withdrawal lasts 165 full payments and one final smaller payment of approximately $155.33 in month 166. That is about 13 years and 10 months from the first monthly period, rather than the 15 years supported by $1,884.05 per month.

A fixed payment that seems affordable at the beginning may still be too high for a long retirement. Run more than one rate and withdrawal scenario; the fixed-rate model does not measure sequence-of-returns risk, where poor early investment performance can undermine later withdrawals.

What Kind of Annuity Are You Modeling?

An annuity is an insurance contract that may accumulate value and provide income immediately or later. Its contractual benefits depend on the insurer and on the precise product. A simple interest-and-withdrawal formula models a balance; it does not price mortality pooling, survivor protection, insurer guarantees, riders, surrender value, or investment-linked benefit bases.

The accumulation phase is the period when premiums or contributions build contract value, often with tax-deferred earnings in a nonqualified annuity. Annuitization is a contractual election that converts value into a stream of income; the available election and degree of reversibility depend on the contract. The payout or distribution phase is when income or withdrawals are received. A flexible withdrawal program from a deferred annuity is not necessarily the same as irrevocably annuitizing it.

Six Common Ways Annuities Pay Income

Fix Length (modeled): Specify a number of years and calculate a level payment. A contract described as “period certain” can have beneficiary provisions that are separate from this balance-only model.

Fix Payment (modeled): Specify the amount withdrawn and estimate the depletion date. Payments from a non-annuitized account are different from lifetime-insured payments.

Lump Sum (not modeled by the two regular-payment formulas): Take an available contract value at once, possibly subject to surrender charges, taxes, withholding, or other contract restrictions.

Life Only (not modeled): Payments continue for one covered person’s life; they ordinarily stop at death unless the contract includes another feature. The amount depends on mortality assumptions and insurer pricing, not only a savings-account rate.

Joint and Survivor (not modeled): Payments continue over two covered lives under specified survivor percentages or terms, generally trading some starting income for survivor protection.

Life with Period Certain (not modeled): Lifetime payments are combined with a minimum period during which a beneficiary may continue receiving payments after an early death, as defined by the contract.

No universal ranking makes one option “best.” Compare guaranteed income, flexibility, legacy goals, beneficiaries, inflation exposure, and the financial strength of the insurer. A life-only estimate cannot be reliably produced by entering a fixed number of years into the balance model.

Taxes: Qualified and Nonqualified Contracts

A qualified annuity sits inside an eligible tax-advantaged retirement arrangement, such as a traditional IRA or employer plan. Distributions generally follow that plan’s tax rules, and pretax amounts are typically taxable as ordinary income. It is not correct to assume every qualified annuity was funded entirely with deductible contributions: after-tax basis and Roth rules can change the treatment. Retirement-plan required minimum distribution (RMD) rules may apply according to account type and owner circumstances.

A nonqualified annuity is generally bought outside a tax-qualified retirement plan with after-tax funds. Its investment in the contract (tax basis) can be recovered tax-free under the applicable rules while taxable earnings are generally ordinary income, not capital gains. A pre-annuitization withdrawal from many post-August 13, 1982 nonqualified contracts generally takes taxable earnings first; periodic annuity payments can instead require an exclusion-ratio calculation under the IRS General Rule. Do not apply earnings-first treatment mechanically to every annuitized payment.

Neither calculator mode estimates tax withholding, exclusion ratios, RMDs, or net after-tax income. Federal tax treatment, state tax, insurance-contract terms, and individual circumstances must be reviewed separately.

Early Withdrawals, Charges, and Exceptions

A withdrawal before age 59 1⁄2 may trigger a 10% additional federal tax on the taxable portion, in addition to ordinary income tax, unless an exception applies. This is not an automatic 10% charge on the entire account balance, and it is separate from an insurer’s contractual surrender charge. Certain substantially equal periodic payments, disability, death, and other circumstances can qualify for statutory exceptions, subject to conditions.

Surrender periods and market-value adjustments can lower cash received if an annuity is surrendered or money moved early. A contract may offer limited free withdrawals, but that does not automatically waive federal income tax or the federal additional tax. Ask for the applicable contract disclosure and review net surrender value rather than equating account value with cash available today.

Replacing an Annuity: Section 1035

A properly structured exchange under Internal Revenue Code Section 1035 can permit an annuity-for-annuity exchange without current recognition of gain when legal requirements are met. A life-insurance contract can, in permitted circumstances, be exchanged for an annuity, but an annuity cannot generally be exchanged tax-free for life insurance. A tax-free exchange is not the same as a fee-free or risk-free exchange.

Partial annuity exchanges are possible under IRS rules. Revenue Procedure 2011-38 provides a 180-day rule for certain direct partial exchanges, with specific exceptions and substance-over-form analysis when its safe conditions are not met. Ownership/annuitant continuity, insurer processing, retained basis, surrender charges, and benefits lost can be decisive. Have the existing and proposed contracts reviewed before transferring value.

What the Estimate Leaves Out

The simplified projection assumes a single starting balance, a specified and steady crediting rate, equal withdrawals, a known frequency, and regular timing. It does not incorporate a mortality table, insurer pricing, inflation-linked increases, income-benefit riders, premium taxes, management or rider fees, state-specific regulation, investment downside, or changes to credited rates. An illustrated gross withdrawal is not the same as a guaranteed contractual income amount or an inflation-adjusted purchasing-power amount.

💡 Pro Tip: For context, $1,884.05 in 15 years does not purchase the same goods as $1,884.05 today if consumer prices rise. Compare inflation-adjusted needs separately; do not simply increase the interest rate to “account for inflation” without changing the spending model. For product decisions, request a current insurer illustration showing guarantees, fees, death benefits, surrender values, and beneficiary terms.
