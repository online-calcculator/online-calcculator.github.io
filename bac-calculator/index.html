Blood Alcohol Concentration (BAC) Calculator

A blood alcohol concentration (BAC) calculator estimates the amount of alcohol in your bloodstream from the drinks you report, your body weight, and the time elapsed. It is an educational estimate, not a breath or blood alcohol test, a diagnosis, or a way to decide whether driving is safe.

BAC is often written as a percentage: 0.08% BAC means 0.08 gram of alcohol per 100 mL of blood, equivalent to 0.08 g/dL. A low estimated number does not mean alcohol has stopped affecting judgment or coordination.

How to use the BAC calculator

The reference calculator asks for gender (male or female), body weight with a selectable weight unit, the hours and minutes since the first drink, and the amount of alcohol consumed. Beverage rows are provided for beer, wine, liquor, and another type of drink. Each row uses the amount, serving size, and alcohol-by-volume (ABV) information needed to estimate ethanol intake.

1. Select the gender option used by the calculator's model. This is a simplified proxy for average differences in alcohol distribution; it does not capture an individual's physiology, gender identity, hormone status, or body composition.

2. Enter your body weight and choose the correct unit. A weight entered in pounds should not be interpreted as kilograms, and vice versa.

3. Enter the time since your first drink, using both hour and minute fields. This is not the same as time since your last drink. The latter affects how much alcohol may still be absorbing.

4. For each beverage type consumed, enter the number of servings and the size and ABV of those servings. Use the 'Other' row for a beverage or pour that does not fit the preset beer, wine, or liquor categories. Count every drink; if you had different strengths of the same beverage, calculate their ethanol amounts separately rather than treating them as identical.

5. Calculate and read the result as an approximate model output. Do not use a predicted BAC, a predicted decline, or a legal threshold as clearance for driving, working at heights, or any other safety-sensitive activity.

The exact dropdown choices, default values, validation limits, and dynamic result presentation may differ between calculator implementations. The instructions above reflect the visible reference form; they do not claim a completed interaction test of our deployed calculator.

What the BAC number tells you

BAC describes alcohol concentration in blood, not the number of drinks in your stomach or how drunk someone feels. Alcohol moves from the digestive tract into the bloodstream, spreads through body water, and is primarily metabolized by the liver. The amount absorbed, distribution across tissues, and the time course of elimination vary considerably.

Online estimates are especially uncertain while drinks are still being absorbed. BAC may continue to rise after a person stops drinking, so a calculated value can lag behind the concentration that develops later. Also, two people with the same reported drinking history can have different measured BAC values.

Drink size, ABV, and U.S. standard drinks

ABV means alcohol by volume: a 5% ABV beverage contains approximately 5 mL of pure ethanol per 100 mL of drink. Serving volume and ABV must be considered together. The U.S. standard drink contains about 14 grams (0.6 U.S. fluid ounce) of pure alcohol, not one glass or one container of any size.

Common examples of about one U.S. standard drink are 12 fl oz of 5% beer, 5 fl oz of 12% wine, and 1.5 fl oz of 40% spirits. By contrast, a 16 fl oz beer at 8% ABV contains approximately 2.1 standard drinks. Mixed drinks and heavy pours can also contain more than one.
