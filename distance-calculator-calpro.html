How to Use the Distance Calculator

Choose the section matching your coordinate type. The 2D and 3D calculators work with any consistent linear units; for example, if all coordinates are in meters, the resulting distance is in meters. Latitude and longitude calculations return both kilometers and miles.

  1. 2D distance: enter the x and y coordinates for both points, then select Calculate. The result includes distance, slope, angle, line equation, and a point diagram.
  2. 3D distance: enter both sets of x, y, and z coordinates. Calculate displays the straight-line distance, differences along each axis, and an isometric diagram.
  3. Latitude and longitude: enter signed decimal degrees (negative for south or west), or select Degree-Minute-Second and use the N/S and E/W directions.
  4. Map distance: click or tap two places to add markers. Drag either marker to change its position. You can also pan, zoom, and clear the map.

Distance Formulas

Distance between two points in 2D

For points (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂), the Euclidean distance is:

d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)²]

Worked example: from (1, 1) to (4, 5), Δx = 3 and Δy = 4, so d = √(3² + 4²) = 5 units. The slope is 4/3 and the direction angle is atan2(4, 3) ≈ 53.13° from the positive x-axis.

Distance between two points in 3D

For points (x₁, y₁, z₁) and (x₂, y₂, z₂):

d = √[(x₂ − x₁)² + (y₂ − y₁)² + (z₂ − z₁)²]

Worked example: the distance from (1, 1, 1) to (2, 2, 2) equals √(1² + 1² + 1²) = √3 ≈ 1.73205 units.

Geographic distance using latitude and longitude

On a spherical Earth, the haversine formula calculates the central angle between two positions. If latitudes φ and longitudes λ are expressed in radians, define:

h = sin²[(φ₂ − φ₁)/2] + cos(φ₁)cos(φ₂)sin²[(λ₂ − λ₁)/2]

Then the approximate great-circle distance is d = 2R atan2(√h, √(1 − h)), where R is the sphere's radius.

For geographic results, this calculator uses the Lambert ellipsoidal approximation, based on reduced latitudes and Earth's flattening (approximately 1/298.257). This follows the method shown in the reference calculator, which accounts for the planet's slight flattening. The model's equatorial radius is 6,378.1 km. Extremely close to antipodal points, where the Lambert expression becomes singular, the calculator switches to a spherical approximation and labels that exception.

Worked example: for (38.8976°, −77.0366°) and (39.9496°, −75.1503°), the calculated distance is approximately 200.0 km or 124.3 miles. These are straight-line surface distances, not road, rail, or flight-route distances.

Converting decimal degrees to DMS

For an angle expressed as degrees, minutes, and seconds, the corresponding magnitude is degrees + minutes/60 + seconds/3600. The N/S or E/W direction determines the sign. Each minute and second value must be less than 60.

Understanding Your Results

The Cartesian calculators report distances in the same units as the input coordinates. A vertical 2D line has an undefined slope, and coincident points do not define a unique line angle. The map and latitude/longitude calculators measure the approximate shortest path over Earth's modeled surface, not a trip through roads or over terrain elevation.

Online OpenStreetMap tiles enhance the interactive map when a network connection is available. If those images cannot load, an embedded Natural Earth world map keeps the marker placement, dragging, zooming, and distance calculations usable offline.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use negative coordinates?

Yes. Negative Cartesian coordinates are valid. For geographic coordinates, latitude ranges from −90° to 90° and longitude from −180° to 180°.

Why is the map distance different from driving distance?

The map uses surface geodesic distance (air distance). Roads and practical routes curve around terrain, coastlines, and other obstacles, so actual travel distance usually differs.

What if both points are the same?

The distance is zero. The 2D calculator explicitly reports that the line slope and orientation are undefined for coincident points.

Are the distances exact?

2D and 3D calculations are direct Euclidean computations subject to ordinary numeric precision. Earth distances are model-based estimates: the real Earth is irregular and is not represented perfectly by a sphere or smooth ellipsoid.
