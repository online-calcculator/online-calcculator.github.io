APR Calculator

An annual percentage rate (APR) puts a loan’s interest rate and certain borrowing charges into one annualized measure. This makes APR useful when comparing loan offers with different origination fees or discount points. It is not the same as the interest rate, monthly payment, or total dollars spent. How to use the APR calculator

  1. Choose the general-loan inputs for a standard loan with a specified compounding and repayment frequency, or the mortgage-specific inputs for a U.S. home loan.
  2. Record fees accurately: a fee paid out of pocket at closing affects the borrower’s initial net proceeds, while a fee added to the loan may raise the principal and scheduled payments.

Do not enter every closing cost as an APR fee; applicable finance-charge rules matter.

General APR: loan inputs and repayment settings

Loan Amount is the amount borrowed; Loan Term is entered in years and months. Interest Rate is the stated annual rate. Compound determines how often interest compounds; Pay Back determines the repayment interval. Loaned Fees and Upfront Fees are separate entries: distinguish charges financed with the loan from charges paid at the outset. Enter numeric amounts rather than percentages unless an interface explicitly requests a percentage. The choice of frequency changes the mathematics, so use the schedule in the actual loan agreement.

The resulting Real APR is an annualized borrowing-cost estimate. Read it alongside Amount Financed, Upfront Out-of-Pocket Fees, the payment amount and frequency, total payments, total interest, and the combined payments and fees. The general tool also exposes an amortization table, which shows how scheduled payments reduce the balance over time. A result based on monthly payments is not interchangeable with one based on weekly or annual payments.

Mortgage APR: U.S. home-loan inputs

House Value is the home price used in the illustration; Down Payment reduces the amount borrowed. Loan Term and Interest Rate specify the mortgage repayment assumptions. Loan Fees are charges assessed in connection with the loan; Points are generally expressed as a percentage of the loan amount, with one discount point equal to 1% of principal. PMI Insurance is entered as an annual amount in the competitor’s mortgage form. Check whether a mortgage insurance payment continues for the whole assumed period: actual cancellation and premium schedules may differ.

The mortgage result includes Real APR, Loan Amount, Down Payment, Monthly Pay, Total of Payments, Total Interest, and All Payments and Fees. Monthly Pay should be read as the modeled mortgage payment, not automatically the full monthly housing expense. Property tax, homeowner’s insurance, association dues, utilities, and maintenance may still have to be budgeted separately. For official lender disclosure comparisons, use the Loan Estimate or Closing Disclosure.

APR versus the stated interest rate

The stated interest rate determines the periodic interest charge on an unpaid loan balance. APR relates the value actually received by the borrower to the timing and size of scheduled payments, while accounting for the charges included under the applicable loan model. If two loans have the same stated rate but one collects an extra qualifying origination fee, that loan normally has a higher APR. A higher APR does not imply that the monthly principal-and-interest payment is higher when the fee is paid separately.

For a fully amortizing fixed-rate loan, each scheduled payment pays that period’s interest first and uses the remainder to reduce principal. As principal falls, the interest portion generally falls and the principal portion generally increases. APR is calculated from the full cash-flow schedule, not simply by adding a fee percentage to the stated rate.

Worked example: a loan with an upfront fee

Assume a hypothetical $20,000 loan at a 7.20% stated annual interest rate, repaid in 48 equal monthly payments, with interest calculated monthly. A $600 qualifying fee is paid at origination and reduces the borrower’s initial net proceeds to $19,400. The repayment amount is still based on the $20,000 principal. This is an illustrative loan, not a lender quote.

Step 1. Compute the scheduled monthly payment

Payment = P × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)^(−N)]

P is the loan principal ($20,000), i is the periodic stated interest rate (0.072 ÷ 12 = 0.006 per month), and N is the number of monthly payments (48). Substituting gives $20,000 × 0.006 ÷ [1 − (1.006)^(−48)] = $480.78 per month, before display rounding.

Step 2. Solve for the fee-adjusted periodic rate

Net proceeds = Payment × [1 − (1 + j)^(−N)] ÷ j

Set net proceeds to $19,400, payment to the unrounded $480.7828768, and N to 48. Solve numerically for j: approximately 0.00732003 per month. For this regular monthly-payment example, the nominal annual APR is 12 × j × 100 = 8.7840%, or 8.78% when rounded to two decimal places. The APR is higher than 7.20% because the borrower receives less cash but must repay the same schedule. Irregular payments, different compounding frequencies, and legal disclosure conventions may require a different calculation.

Step 3. Interpret the total cost

Forty-eight unrounded payments total about $23,077.58. Subtracting the $20,000 principal gives $3,077.58 in scheduled interest. Adding the $600 upfront fee brings modeled interest and qualifying fee costs to $3,677.58; aggregate scheduled payments plus that fee are $23,677.58. The APR is a rate, not the dollar finance charge. Real lender schedules may round each payment to cents and adjust the final payment.

Understanding the repayment schedule and result labels

The loan amount or amount financed is the principal basis used in the selected model; cash actually received can be smaller when fees are withheld. “Total of Payments” adds scheduled installment payments, while “Total Interest” isolates their interest component in the modeled amortization. “All Payments and Fees” combines modeled payments with the listed charges under the tool’s convention; it is not a universal statement of all living or ownership costs. Keep out-of-pocket cash, financed charges, and regular insurance payments distinct to avoid double-counting.

Which fees belong in APR?

For U.S. closed-end consumer credit, Regulation Z defines finance charges and certain exclusions; the details depend on the product and transaction. Charges that commonly contribute include lender origination charges, borrower-paid points, mortgage broker charges, and certain required insurance costs. A lender’s legally disclosed APR may differ from a simple online approximation because the financing rules, payment dates, mortgage insurance, and categorization of each charge may differ.

When a lower APR may not be the cheaper choice

An APR comparison assumes the scheduled loan cash flows follow the disclosed terms. A borrower who sells, refinances, or pays off early may spread points and upfront costs across fewer years. For example, a lower-rate mortgage obtained by purchasing points might save money over a long horizon but fail to recover the upfront cost over a short holding period. Compare cash-to-close, the monthly savings, the remaining balance, and total interest plus fees over the period you actually expect to keep the loan.

For mortgages, compare Loan Estimates for the same loan type, loan amount, term, rate-lock assumptions, and similar points or credits. Look at the loan’s “In 5 years” comparison as one scenario, not as a universal holding period. APR comparisons become less straightforward when terms vary substantially, such as fixed versus adjustable rates, balloon payments, or loans with differing mortgage-insurance timing.

Fixed APR and variable APR loans

A fixed-rate amortizing loan generally maintains a constant contractual interest rate and predictable scheduled principal-and-interest payment over its term. Its disclosed APR can still exceed the contract rate because of included charges. A variable-rate loan can change with an index and a contractual margin, subject to its reset timing, caps, floors, and other terms. The initial disclosed APR is not a ceiling on future cost. Review the index, margin, initial and lifetime adjustment caps, possible maximum payment, and the lender’s loan disclosures before deciding.

A low initial variable rate may later rise, remain steady, or fall. Neither direction can be presumed from a market forecast. Comparing initial APRs alone can hide meaningful differences in future payment risk.

APR versus APY and effective annual rates

APR and APY answer different questions. For a straightforward nominal rate quoted with a periodic compounding frequency, the effective annual rate (often called APY in the context of savings deposits) captures the effect of earning interest on interest over one year. The closed-end loan APR is not automatically an effective yield: under the U.S. actuarial disclosure convention, the regular periodic rate is annualized by multiplying by the number of periods per year.

Effective annual rate = (1 + r / m)^m − 1

Here r is a nominal annual rate in decimal form and m is the number of compounding periods per year. At a 7.20% nominal annual rate compounded monthly, (1 + 0.072/12)^12 − 1 ≈ 7.4424%. This is a compounding conversion, not the fee-adjusted 8.78% APR in the worked loan example. For a fee-bearing loan, the relevant cash-flow rate has to be calculated separately.

Practical checks and limitations

💡 Pro Tip: Before relying on a result, verify the loan term, exact payment dates and frequency, contract interest-rate convention, treatment of loaned versus upfront charges, points as a percent or dollar amount, mortgage insurance amount and duration, and whether the loan uses fixed or adjustable terms. This simplified article calculation assumes regular end-of-month payments without late fees, prepayment penalties, changing rates, or cash advances. An APR is not a guarantee of approval, actual repayment, or future rate behavior.

An unusual or very short loan, zero-interest loan with mandatory fees, promotional-rate financing, or irregular payment schedule can still have an APR different from the simple examples here. For an actual U.S. credit offer, rely on the lender’s required disclosures and ask for an itemized explanation if an online estimate disagrees.
