What this calculator does and does not do

The tool on this page addresses the accumulation phase: money is invested or credited interest before withdrawals or annuity payments begin. It provides an end balance, starting principal, total additions, total return/interest earned, and month-by-month and year-by-year schedules. This tool does not determine a lifetime income quote, a fixed-length payout, an annuitization rate, tax owed, surrender penalties, or a contract-specific guaranteed minimum. Those require different information and often a separate payout calculator or insurer illustration.

Despite the word 'annuity,' the calculation is a regular-deposit compound-growth model. It can illustrate saving patterns even when the financial product you are evaluating has complicated guarantees, fees, caps, or market risk that the formula cannot reproduce.

How to enter the six important settings

Starting principal

Enter the dollar amount already in the annuity at the beginning of your projection. For example, $15,000 represents funds present before any new recurring deposits. Do not add that opening amount again to the monthly or annual contribution boxes.

Annual addition

💡 Tip: Enter the amount you plan to add once every year. A $2,400 annual addition means $2,400 each year, not $200 per month. If you also enter a monthly addition, the two are combined; avoid double-counting the same planned contribution.

Monthly addition

Enter the amount deposited every month. A $200 monthly addition adds $2,400 each year, separate from any annual addition. This input can represent a fixed savings plan, but real insurance contracts may set minimum premiums, maximum premiums, or other contribution rules.

Add at each period's beginning or end

Beginning (annuity due): each monthly deposit is made before that month's growth, and the annual deposit is made at the start of its year. End (ordinary/immediate annuity): deposits are made after the period's growth, with the annual payment at year-end. An earlier deposit is invested for more time and therefore usually produces a higher ending balance when the rate is positive. The phrase 'ordinary/immediate annuity' in this mathematical timing setting is not the same as an insurance-company immediate income annuity.

Annual growth rate

Enter an assumed annual percentage growth rate, such as 5%, not 5 as a decimal. This is a projection rate; it is not automatically a contract rate, annual percentage yield, guarantee, or return after all insurer expenses. Annuity crediting rates can change or depend on indexes, participation rates, spreads, caps, floors, investments, or riders. Use a carefully defined net rate only if it is appropriate to the model.

After (years)

Enter the projection horizon in years. Ten years means 120 monthly periods; 12 years means 144 monthly periods. The time period should be chosen separately from the date on which an insurance contract permits penalty-free access.

How the accumulation calculation works

The monthly model behind the schedules

The calculator's visible example is consistent with a monthly rate derived from an effective annual growth assumption: rₘ = (1 + R)^(1/12) − 1, where R is the annual rate as a decimal and rₘ is the monthly growth factor minus one. With R = 5%, rₘ ≈ 0.004074124, or 0.4074124% per month. Applying 5% ÷ 12 instead would describe a different annual-rate convention and yield a somewhat different balance.

Let Bₘ₋₁ be the previous month's ending balance, Dₘ the month's deposits (monthly addition plus any annual addition due in that month), and Bₘ the new ending balance. For beginning-of-period contributions: Bₘ = (Bₘ₋₁ + Dₘ) × (1 + rₘ). For end-of-period contributions: Bₘ = Bₘ₋₁ × (1 + rₘ) + Dₘ. The interest/return earned in a month is the difference attributable to the rate, not the amount deposited. Each recurrence is repeated for every month in the chosen horizon.

For a single uniform end-of-period payment A each period, the future value of an ordinary annuity is FV = A × [((1 + r)^n − 1) / r], provided r ≠ 0. For beginning-of-period payments, multiply that payment stream's value by (1 + r). A starting principal P independently grows to P × (1 + r)^n. When monthly and annual additions coexist, applying the monthly recurrence is clearer than pretending all additions are one identical payment stream. When r = 0, the total is simply P plus all deposits.

A complete worked example: $15,000 plus monthly and yearly deposits

Assume an initial balance of $15,000, an annual deposit of $2,400, a separate monthly deposit of $200, a 5% effective annual growth rate, and a 12-year accumulation horizon. Choose beginning-of-period deposits and assume the rate applies evenly each month. In month 1 the $2,400 annual addition and $200 monthly addition are invested immediately: ($15,000 + $2,600) × 1.004074124 = $17,671.70. Of that first month's balance, $71.70 is modeled growth.

By the end of year 1, the model reaches $20,734.52. Across 12 years, the total deposits are 12 × $2,400 + 144 × $200 = $57,600. Therefore, the ending balance is $106,277.01, consisting of $15,000 starting principal + $57,600 deposits + $33,677.01 of modeled return. This is a constant-rate, no-fee illustration, not a claim that an actual contract will credit 5% each year.

Interpret the year-by-year accumulation schedule

Each year summarizes the deposits made during that year, the modeled growth accrued during that year, and its ending balance. The next year's ending balance builds on the previous balance. The exact dollar value at each checkpoint depends on the monthly rate and when deposits occur; it must not be reconstructed by adding a flat annual interest amount.

Beginning-of-period versus end-of-period deposits

Holding all other assumptions fixed, the end-of-period option gives each payment less time to compound. In the same 12-year example, beginning-of-period deposits produce $106,277.01, compared with $104,207.78 when deposits are made at the ends of their periods. The difference is $2,069.23. That gap reflects timing, not a higher stated interest rate.

Understand every result instead of relying on the headline balance

End balance is the calculated accumulation at the end of the chosen horizon. Starting principal is the opening balance you entered. Total additions is the sum of new annual and monthly deposits; it excludes starting principal. Total return/interest earned is the balancing component: ending value minus starting principal minus total additions. A negative return would be possible under an investment-loss scenario, but a simple constant-positive-rate model does not capture real market volatility.

A monthly schedule helps audit deposit timing and growth within each year. A yearly schedule reveals long-term compounding. Do not interpret either table as an official insurance illustration or a statement of cash surrender value: contract-level features, fees, and taxes can materially alter the proceeds.

What is an annuity in the United States?

An annuity is a contract between its owner and an insurance company. The buyer pays an initial premium, subsequent premiums, or both. In return, the insurer provides the contract's specified accumulation and/or income benefits. The owner controls contractual choices, while the annuitant is the person whose life may determine life-contingent payments. Owner, annuitant, and beneficiary can be different people, depending on the policy. Terms govern ownership rights, death benefits, withdrawals, and any payout election.

Annuities may be used to accumulate assets with deferred taxation, create future income, protect a stated minimum under specified conditions, or combine several features. They are not interchangeable with a bank deposit, a mutual fund, or a pension. Guarantees are obligations of the issuing insurer and depend on its claims-paying ability, not on this calculator's projection.

The accumulation phase versus the income phase

During accumulation, deposits and credited returns or investment performance affect the contract's value. In the payout phase, the contract pays income or allows distributions under its terms. Some contracts are annuitized: the value is converted to a payment promise, often with an irrevocable election. Others allow systematic withdrawals while maintaining an account value. This page estimates only the first phase. It cannot tell you the lifetime monthly income an insurer would offer.

Fixed, variable, and indexed annuities

Annuities are often grouped by how value grows. A fixed annuity generally credits interest according to its contract, possibly with a minimum guarantee. Some fixed annuities specify a multi-year guaranteed rate (MYGA), whereas other renewal rates can change after an initial guarantee. The protection is contract-specific; surrender value can still be reduced by fees or adjustments.

A variable annuity commonly offers investment subaccounts whose values rise or fall with securities markets. Optional income/death-benefit riders may create certain guarantees, but basic investment value and returns are generally not guaranteed. The expense structure can include investment expenses, mortality-and-expense risk charges, administrative fees, and optional rider costs.

An indexed annuity typically credits interest using an external index-based formula rather than giving investors direct ownership of the index. Participation rates, caps, spreads, floors, averaging methods, and contract renewals can limit the credit or change the outcome. A 10% rise in an index does not mean a contract necessarily credits 10%, and an indexed crediting formula is not the same as the constant 5% rate in our illustrative schedule.

Immediate versus deferred annuities

A deferred annuity generally has a period before income begins, often allowing accumulated value to build over years. An immediate annuity typically converts a single premium into periodic income starting within a relatively short time, commonly within a year. The fixed/variable/indexed distinction describes how value or payments are determined; immediate/deferred describes when income starts. These classifications answer different questions, so a product may be described using more than one.

Income and beneficiary choices to know about

When comparing the eventual payout phase, common designs include life-only income, a life annuity with a guaranteed period, joint-and-survivor income, a fixed number of payments, and certain refund or death-benefit features. Payment sizes and beneficiary protection vary by contract. A life-only choice may pay more per month but stop at death; joint-and-survivor income can continue to a survivor and is often priced differently. The accumulation calculator does not price or guarantee any of these options.

Benefits and trade-offs to evaluate

Potential attractions include tax-deferred growth in an eligible nonqualified contract, an option to convert assets into a predictable income stream, and certain protections or death benefits when provided by the contract. Some nonqualified annuities have no general IRS annual premium limit comparable to IRA or 401(k) elective-deferral limits, but the insurer may limit premiums, and qualified-account contribution rules still apply.

Important drawbacks are complexity, potential fees and surrender charges, limited liquidity, restrictions on changing an annuitization election, inflation erosion of fixed payments, and insurer-credit or investment risk. A feature providing a guarantee often comes with conditions or costs. Choosing an annuity purely because its illustration has the largest future account balance can miss these trade-offs.

Fees, surrender charges, and liquidity

Fees may be charged directly, deducted from invested assets, embedded in index-crediting terms, or reflected in the cost of optional features. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point; 100 basis points equals 1%. A quoted gross growth rate and a net rate after charges should never be treated as the same input.

Surrendering a contract

Surrender means terminating a contract for its available cash surrender value. A deferred annuity may impose charges that decline during a surrender-charge period; some apply separate schedules to later premiums. Market value adjustments, recapture of benefits, and tax consequences may also apply. A stated account value can therefore exceed the amount payable if the owner exits early. There is no universal five-, eight-, or ten-year schedule to read the specific policy.

Many contracts provide a state- or policy-specific free-look period after delivery; qualifying cancellations can follow different refund rules. Do not assume a universal 10-to-30-day period or a full premium refund without checking the signed contract and applicable state rules.

U.S. tax treatment: qualified versus nonqualified annuities

Nonqualified annuities

A nonqualified annuity is generally purchased with after-tax funds outside a tax-qualified retirement plan. Its earnings typically grow tax-deferred rather than tax-free; taxable income is recognized under applicable distribution rules. Before the annuity starting date, many nonqualified withdrawals are treated as taking taxable earnings first, then recovering investment in the contract, subject to important grandfathering and special rules.

Once qualifying periodic annuity payments begin, part of each payment may be excludable as a return of the owner's after-tax investment and part may be taxable as ordinary income. For nonqualified annuities, the IRS General Rule and its exclusion-ratio approach can apply. An accumulation balance alone cannot determine those fractions; contractual expected returns, annuitant age, tax basis, and payout terms are needed.

Qualified annuities and retirement account rollovers

A qualified annuity is funded or held under an eligible tax-advantaged retirement arrangement, such as an IRA or employer plan, subject to the rules of that arrangement. Contributions and withdrawals follow the retirement account's tax treatment rather than a blanket rule that every qualified annuity dollar is pretax. If the annuity is held within an already tax-deferred IRA or 401(k), the annuity does not add an extra layer of income-tax deferral; its other insurance features must justify any added cost.

Eligible direct rollovers or trustee-to-trustee transfers can preserve tax treatment when the receiving arrangement is eligible and the process is correctly handled. The 60-day deadline generally matters for qualifying indirect rollovers, not properly completed direct transfers. The one-rollover-per-year rule applies to certain IRA-to-IRA rollovers; it is not a universal limit on qualified plan-to-IRA direct rollovers. A rollover or conversion to Roth tax treatment may produce taxable income, depending on the transaction.

Early distributions and the additional federal tax

The IRS generally imposes an additional 10% federal tax on the taxable portion of many distributions from qualified plans and nonqualified annuity contracts before age 59½, unless a relevant exception applies. Some exceptions concern immediate annuity contracts, substantially equal periodic payments, disability, or particular circumstances; they have detailed requirements. Being younger than 59½ does not by itself prevent every possible legal payout, and waiting until that age does not guarantee that a contract's surrender fees disappear.

Income tax, additional federal tax, insurer surrender charges, and any state taxes are distinct costs. A projection showing $106,277 of accumulated value cannot tell you how much cash you would keep after any of these charges. Review the policy and IRS instructions before a distribution.

Section 1035 exchanges

Under Internal Revenue Code Section 1035, a qualifying annuity-to-annuity exchange may continue tax deferral without currently recognizing the contract's gain, provided the applicable ownership, transfer, and other requirements are met. Do not assume that taking the proceeds personally and depositing them into another policy qualifies. Exchanges may reset surrender periods, change guarantees, and cause fees; a tax-free exchange can still be economically disadvantageous.

How to make a useful comparison without overtrusting a projection

First match the deposit-timing option to your actual payment schedule. Second check whether the annual growth rate is a guaranteed contract credit, a hypothetical return, an index-crediting example, or an investment assumption. Third account for explicit fees, embedded caps/spreads, premium loads, and possible surrender adjustments. Fourth, use more than one rate and time horizon. Finally compare the accumulated value with a separately obtained insurer income quote when your real objective is retirement cash flow.

A simple sensitivity exercise is to recalculate the same deposits at 3%, 5%, and 7% effective annual growth, noting that these are scenarios rather than predicted returns. Another useful comparison holds the rate fixed but selects beginning versus end of period. If the insurer's contract has a guarantee, compare the guaranteed minimum illustration with any non-guaranteed current-rate illustration rather than treating either as a certain outcome.
