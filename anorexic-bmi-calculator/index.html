Anorexic BMI Calculator

This calculator estimates body mass index (BMI) from height and weight and puts the number in the context of possible low body weight. BMI is a screening measurement, not a test for anorexia nervosa. A person can have an eating disorder at any body size, and an unexpectedly low BMI can have causes other than an eating disorder. Use the number as a starting point for understanding a measurement, not as a verdict about someone’s health or a weight to aim for. If eating, weight changes, or body-image concerns are causing distress, a qualified healthcare professional can assess the whole picture.

How to use the calculator

  1. Choose the measurement system that matches your measurements. Depending on the available interface, unit choices may include US units, metric units, and an additional unit-selection tab.
  2. Enter age and the requested sex/gender selection, then enter height and weight in the displayed units. In US units, the visible fields use feet, inches, and pounds; the metric form uses centimeters and kilograms.
  3. Select Calculate to see the BMI result.

💡 Tip: Use measured height without shoes and a reasonably recent weight. Check the unit labels before calculating: for example, kilograms and pounds are not interchangeable. The mathematical BMI formula is the same for all adults; age and sex become especially important when interpreting BMI during childhood and adolescence.

What the result means

The principal output is a BMI value in kg/m². A result message may say whether the number appears consistent with the calculator’s low-BMI screening rule, and the page may display an adult reference range. These are screening labels, not diagnostic findings. A message that the result does not suggest anorexia cannot exclude restrictive eating, atypical anorexia, or another eating disorder.

How BMI is calculated

BMI expresses weight in relation to height squared. With metric measurements, BMI = weight in kilograms ÷ (height in meters)². With US customary measurements, BMI = 703 × weight in pounds ÷ (height in inches)². Convert centimeters to meters by dividing by 100; convert feet to inches by multiplying feet by 12 and adding the extra inches.

Example (an illustrative adult measurement, not a target): for 70 kg and 1.75 m, BMI = 70 ÷ (1.75 × 1.75) = 70 ÷ 3.0625 ≈ 22.86 kg/m². The same BMI is obtained using consistent customary units, apart from minor rounding. BMI describes a ratio; it does not directly measure fat percentage, muscle, nutritional deficiencies, or mental health.

Adult BMI categories and clinical meaning

For adults age 20 and older, CDC classifies BMI below 18.5 kg/m² as underweight, 18.5 to below 25 as healthy weight, 25 to below 30 as overweight, and 30 or higher as obesity. These are population-level screening categories; an individual’s health requires context. Neither the 18.5 threshold nor a lower historical cutoff such as 17.5 establishes an anorexia diagnosis.

Anorexia nervosa is assessed from a combination of eating restriction, low weight in the relevant developmental and health context, fear of weight gain or behaviors interfering with weight restoration, changes in the experience of body weight or shape, and the person’s physical and mental health. Clinicians also consider rapid weight loss and whether the person has failed to gain expected weight during growth.

Why BMI-based severity labels require caution

Some psychiatric diagnostic references provide adult BMI-based severity specifiers after anorexia nervosa has already been diagnosed. The minimum specifier is not the same as medical stability or the need for treatment. Clinical symptoms, functioning, rate of weight change, vital signs, and other findings can indicate greater severity at any BMI. The numeric boundaries below are clinical descriptors, not goals or standalone diagnostic tests.

In particular, “mild” in a BMI-based classification does not mean harmless, and no particular BMI alone reliably predicts the onset of organ damage. The medical risk of malnutrition cannot be reduced to a single exact number. NICE specifically advises against using a single BMI measurement to decide whether treatment or hospital care is needed.

Children and teenagers need BMI-for-age interpretation

An adult BMI cutoff should not be applied mechanically to a child or teenager. For ages 2 through 19, CDC interprets BMI using age- and sex-specific growth-chart percentiles. A value below the 5th percentile is classified as underweight in that screening system, but growth history and development are essential to interpretation. A child may need assessment because of slowed growth or failure to gain expected weight even without an obvious decline on a scale.

If the calculator displays only a raw BMI number or a simplified anorexia warning, do not substitute that message for a pediatric BMI-for-age percentile or clinical assessment. An age-appropriate growth-chart calculator and a pediatric healthcare professional are more suitable for interpreting the number.

Why the result cannot diagnose or rule out an eating disorder

Eating disorders affect people of different ages, sexes, and body weights. Someone whose BMI lies in a usual adult reference range may still experience significant restriction, intense fear related to weight or shape, rapid weight change, or health complications. Atypical anorexia is an example in which anorexia-like symptoms and substantial weight loss occur without low weight by standard cutoffs. Conversely, lower weight may arise from another illness, medication effect, or an individual health circumstance.

A clinical evaluation may cover eating patterns, exercise, weight history, distress about eating or body image, medical history, vital signs, and a physical examination. Depending on symptoms, a clinician may order blood tests or an electrocardiogram and assess co-occurring anxiety, depression, or other concerns. No single questionnaire, BMI reading, or laboratory test replaces this assessment.

Health concerns and when to seek help

Signs worth discussing with a healthcare professional include persistent food restriction, distress around meals, fear of weight gain, frequent weight-checking, compulsive exercise, purging behaviors, rapid weight loss, dizziness, unusual fatigue, missed or delayed periods, and withdrawal from social situations involving food. Eating disorders can affect the heart, bones, digestion, hormones, and psychological well-being.

Seek prompt professional assessment if you suspect an eating disorder, regardless of the BMI result. Fainting, chest pain, serious weakness, confusion, a markedly irregular heartbeat, or immediate safety concerns warrant urgent medical attention. Treatment may involve coordinated medical monitoring, psychological care, and nutritional support; early intervention matters.

Limitations and measurement tips

💡 Tip: BMI is sensitive to the accuracy of height and weight measurements and to the units selected. A small error in height can change the result because height is squared. Rounding a value near a category boundary may change the displayed category without indicating a meaningful change in health. Compare measurements taken consistently rather than focusing on a hundredth of a BMI unit.

BMI also cannot reveal muscle mass, body composition, hydration status, eating behaviors, or the trajectory of recent weight change. Standard adult BMI categories are not a substitute for interpretation during growth or pregnancy, or for someone whose medical situation changes how weight should be evaluated. A clinician can explain when additional measurements are more informative.
