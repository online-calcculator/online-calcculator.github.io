What Is the Heat Index?

The heat index estimates how hot the air feels when humidity is considered alongside air temperature. When humidity is high, perspiration evaporates less effectively, making it harder for the body to cool itself. This is an estimate for shaded conditions with light wind; direct sunshine can make the perceived heat substantially greater.

How to Use the Heat Index Calculator

  1. For a known humidity percentage, use Relative Humidity: enter the air temperature, choose Celsius, Fahrenheit, or Kelvin, and enter relative humidity from 0 to 100%.
  2. When you know the dew point instead, use Dew Point Temperature: enter air temperature and dew point, selecting the unit of each separately.
  3. Click Calculate under the relevant method. The heat index and caution category will appear above that method's input form.
  4. Use Save in the result heading to download a plain-text summary, or Clear to erase that method's inputs and result.

Heat Index Formula

This calculator follows the U.S. National Weather Service two-stage calculation. First, it calculates the simplified Steadman estimate. If its average with the air temperature reaches 80°F, it uses the Rothfusz regression and applies any relevant adjustment for very low or very high humidity.

Rothfusz regression (temperatures in °F):

HI = −42.379 + 2.04901523T + 10.14333127R − 0.22475541TR − 0.00683783T² − 0.05481717R² + 0.00122874T²R + 0.00085282TR² − 0.00000199T²R²

Here, T is air temperature in degrees Fahrenheit, and R is relative humidity as a percentage. The low- and high-humidity adjustment terms follow the NWS/WPC method.

Calculating Relative Humidity from Dew Point

If dew point is supplied, relative humidity is estimated with the Magnus vapor-pressure approximation, using Celsius temperatures:

RH = 100 × exp[17.62Td / (243.12 + Td) − 17.62T / (243.12 + T)]

Here, Td is dew point and T is air temperature. Dew point should not exceed the air temperature under ordinary, non-supersaturated conditions.

Worked Heat Index Examples

Example 1: Relative humidity

At 30°C (86°F) and 70% relative humidity, the estimated heat index is about 35°C (95°F), in the Extreme caution range.

Example 2: Dew point

At an air temperature of 30°C and a dew point of 22°C, the Magnus formula gives approximately 62% relative humidity. The resulting heat index is about 33°C (92°F), also in the Extreme caution range.

Heat Index Risk Levels

These categories indicate general heat-related risk during prolonged exposure or physical activity. Personal vulnerability and circumstances can change the actual risk.

Heat indexRisk levelPossible effects
80–89°F (27–31°C)CautionFatigue may occur with sustained heat exposure.
90–104°F (32–40°C)Extreme cautionHeat cramps and heat exhaustion may occur.
105–129°F (41–54°C)DangerHeat cramps or exhaustion are likely; heat stroke is possible.
130°F+ (54°C+)Extreme dangerHeat stroke poses an immediate threat.
Safety note: A heat-index value is not an individual medical prediction. Outdoor work, direct sun, exertion, dehydration, and other factors can increase danger. Stop strenuous activity and seek cooling if you develop heat-related symptoms.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is heat index the same as air temperature?

No. Air temperature is measured directly. Heat index combines temperature and humidity to approximate the apparent temperature experienced by people.

Can I use Fahrenheit or Kelvin?

Yes. All temperature fields support Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin. Changing a field's unit automatically converts its existing numeric temperature.

What if the air temperature is below 80°F?

The calculator returns the simplified NWS estimate when appropriate. The full Rothfusz regression is generally not used at lower apparent temperatures, and ordinary heat-risk thresholds are not applicable.

Why can sunshine feel hotter than the result?

The official heat-index approximation describes shady conditions and light wind. Direct sunlight can raise apparent heat by up to about 15°F, according to the National Weather Service.
