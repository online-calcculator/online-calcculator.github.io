How to use the resistor calculator

This page combines four practical electrical-resistance tools. For resistor color bands, choose whether the part has three, four, five, or six bands, then select each band color in order from the left. The diagram updates as you select colors; click Calculate to display the nominal resistance, tolerance range, and, for six-band parts, temperature coefficient.

To find equivalent resistance, enter resistor values separated by commas in the parallel or series box. Values are assumed to be in ohms (Ω), with optional k, M, or G suffixes. To compute the resistance of a round conductor, enter its length and diameter, select their units, and choose the material or type a custom electrical conductivity in siemens per meter (S/m).

Resistor color code: digits, multipliers, and tolerance

The resistor color code is standardized by IEC 60062. Three- and four-band resistors use two significant digits followed by a multiplier. A four-band resistor adds a tolerance band; a three-band part without a marked tolerance uses ±20%. Five- and six-band resistors use three significant digits, followed by the multiplier and tolerance. The sixth band conventionally indicates temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR), measured in ppm/K.

Resistance = significant digits × color multiplier

Worked color-band example

For a four-band resistor colored green, red, blue, gold, the first two bands represent 5 and 2. Blue has a multiplier of 106, giving 52 × 106 Ω = 52 MΩ. Gold indicates ±5% tolerance, so the expected range is 49.4–54.6 MΩ. The calculator defaults to this example.

Resistors connected in parallel

Components in parallel share the same two connection nodes. Total conductance is the sum of the individual conductances, so the equivalent resistance of positive resistances is less than the smallest branch resistance. If an ideal branch has zero resistance, the equivalent resistance is zero.

1 / Rtotal = 1 / R1 + 1 / R2 + … + 1 / Rn

Example: a 10 Ω resistor and a 20 Ω resistor in parallel have an equivalent resistance of 1 / (1/10 + 1/20) = 6.666666… Ω.

Resistors connected in series

Series-connected resistors carry the same current. Their equivalent resistance is simply the sum of the resistances:

Rtotal = R1 + R2 + … + Rn

Example: 10 Ω, 20 Ω, and 30 Ω in series total 60 Ω. The reference values 10, 2, 38, 23, 38, 23, and 21 Ω total 155 Ω.

Resistance of a round conductor

The DC resistance of a straight conductor with a uniform circular cross-section is calculated from its length, diameter, and electrical conductivity:

R = L / (σ × A)   where   A = π(d/2)2

Here, R is resistance in ohms (Ω), L is length in meters, σ is conductivity in S/m, A is cross-sectional area in square meters, and d is diameter in meters. A longer wire increases resistance, a thicker wire reduces resistance, and a more conductive material reduces resistance.

Worked example: the default values use 100 feet of copper wire, a diameter of 0.05 inches, and conductivity 59,600,000 S/m. This yields approximately 0.40371174065481 Ω. Conductivity values are reference inputs and vary with temperature, alloy, and processing.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between four- and five-band resistors?

Four-band codes have two significant digits, a multiplier, and a tolerance. Five-band codes have three significant digits, which supports more precise nominal values.

What does a sixth resistor band mean?

A sixth band most commonly gives the temperature coefficient (TCR) in parts per million per kelvin. An indicated 100 ppm/K corresponds to a change of approximately 0.01% per kelvin near the rated reference temperature.

Can equivalent resistance be zero?

Yes. In an ideal parallel circuit, a zero-ohm branch shorts the two common nodes, making the equivalent resistance zero. Series resistance is zero only if every series component is zero ohms.

Why might measured wire resistance be different?

The circular-wire formula assumes a uniform material, constant cross-section, and a specified conductivity. Temperature, contact resistance, connector losses, and alternating-current skin effect can change actual measurements.
