What Is an APR Calculator?

The annual percentage rate (APR) estimates the annualized cost of borrowing. Unlike the stated interest rate, APR also accounts for finance charges such as qualifying lender fees and mortgage points. Use the general loan section to compare installment loans with different compounding and repayment frequencies, or use the mortgage section to estimate the annual cost of a home loan.

How to Use the APR Calculator

  1. Choose the general loan or mortgage calculator.
  2. Enter the amount or home value, term, and stated annual interest rate.
  3. Enter any fees paid at closing or added to the loan. For a mortgage, enter lender points and any annual PMI premium.
  4. Select compounding and payment frequencies for a general loan. Mortgage estimates assume monthly compounding and payments.
  5. Click Calculate to update APR, payments, total interest, cost breakdown and chart. Open View Amortization Table to inspect individual installments; download the schedule as CSV if needed.

APR Formulas and Assumptions

For an installment loan, the principal-and-interest payment is M = B × i ÷ [1 − (1 + i)−n], where B is the balance including financed fees, i is the effective interest rate per payment period and n is the number of payments. At 0% interest, M = B ÷ n. If interest compounds m times per year and payments occur p times per year, the payment-period rate is i = (1 + r/m)m/p − 1 for nominal annual rate r. For continuous compounding, i = er/p − 1.

APR is obtained numerically from the cash flow equation Net proceeds = Σ [Paymentt ÷ (1 + j)t], where j is the implied rate per payment interval. The annual percentage rate displayed is 100 × j × p. Mortgage calculations use monthly periods and account for point charges and the entered PMI schedule. This reflects the regular-period actuarial approach described in Regulation Z, Appendix J; actual lender disclosures may differ because of irregular payment dates and fee classification.

General APR Worked Example

Borrow $100,000 for 10 years at 6% nominal annual interest, compounded and paid monthly, with $2,500 in upfront fees. The regular payment is about $1,110.21, total interest is about $33,224.60, and the proceeds after upfront fees are $97,500. Discounting the 120 monthly payments to those proceeds gives an estimated 6.563% APR.

Mortgage APR Worked Example

A $350,000 home with a 20% down payment has a $280,000 loan. At 6.2% for 30 years, the principal-and-interest payment is about $1,714.91 each month. With $3,500 in loan fees and 0.5 points (another $1,400) and no PMI, the estimated APR is 6.367%.

Understanding Loan Fees, Points and PMI

Financed loan fees increase the balance on which interest accrues. Upfront fees are paid at closing and reduce the effective cash proceeds for APR purposes. One mortgage point is 1% of the loan balance. The mortgage model assumes any entered annual PMI is paid in monthly installments until the scheduled principal balance reaches 78% of the original home value; this is a planning simplification, not a universal lender rule.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my APR higher than my interest rate?

Upfront finance charges and financed lender fees increase the economic cost of borrowing. That increase is captured by the APR, even if your contractual interest rate stays the same.

Can an APR be different with weekly versus monthly payments?

Yes. The total number of payments, the length of each payment period and how often interest compounds change both the periodic payment and the equivalent annualized borrowing cost.

Does a lower APR always mean a lower monthly payment?

No. A loan with lower APR can still have a larger payment because of a shorter term, different principal, or different fees. Compare loans with similar amounts and terms and consider total lifetime costs.

Does the calculator include taxes or homeowner insurance?

No. The mortgage estimate excludes property taxes, homeowner insurance and escrow payments. The optional PMI field is separate. Such costs may affect your housing budget without necessarily being part of the legally disclosed APR.

Why might my lender's disclosed APR differ?

A lender may use exact first-payment dates, different classifications of finance charges, adjustable rates, PMI rules or other details that this fixed-rate, regular-payment model does not cover. Use the lender's official Loan Estimate and disclosures for financing decisions.
