How to Use the Roth IRA Calculator

Enter your current Roth IRA balance, yearly contribution, expected annual return, current age, retirement age, and marginal tax rate. Select Maximize contributions — Yes to use 2026 IRA contribution limits instead of a fixed annual contribution. Click Calculate to update the comparison table, balance accumulation graph, and year-by-year schedule. Click Clear to empty the inputs, Print to print the projection, or Save CSV to export a detailed schedule.

How the Roth IRA Projection Works

The estimate assumes annual compounding, a constant annual return, and deposits made at the end of each year. The Roth IRA balance grows without modeled taxes on investment earnings. A comparable taxable account pays the chosen marginal tax rate on positive annual investment returns. Both accounts receive the same annual contributions in this simplified comparison.

Formulas

For annual return r, annual contribution C, and marginal tax rate t (each rate expressed as a decimal), the model calculates:

  • Roth IRA at year-end: Roth start × (1 + r) + C.
  • Taxable account at year-end: Taxable start × (1 + r) + C − [max(0, Taxable start × r) × t].
  • Principal: Starting balance + sum of all annual contributions.
  • Total gross interest: Roth ending balance − principal; taxable ending balance + taxes paid − principal.

Annual contributions do not earn interest until the next year. The table labels each row by the age at the start of that year. The model does not assume a tax credit for investment losses, future changes in market returns, inflation, or withdrawals.

Worked Example

With a $30,000 starting balance, $7,500 contributed at the end of each year, a 6% annual return, a 25% marginal tax rate, and 35 years from age 30 to 65, the first-year Roth IRA balance is $39,300, and the taxable balance is $38,850. At age 65 the estimated Roth IRA balance is $1,066,343 versus $751,245 in the taxable account, a difference of approximately $315,098. Principal totals $292,500; rounded Roth interest is $773,843.

2026 Roth IRA Contribution Limits

For 2026, the U.S. Internal Revenue Service lists an IRA contribution limit of $7,500 for those under 50 and $8,600 for those 50 or older, subject to earned income and other eligibility rules. The Maximize contributions option uses those nominal 2026 amounts at every projected age without predicting future IRS limit changes. The calculator does not test income-based Roth IRA phaseouts, filing status, or other tax circumstances. Manually entered contributions above the illustrated 2026 limits will trigger a notice but remain calculable as a hypothetical scenario.

Understanding the Results

Balance is the projected account value at retirement. Total principal consists of starting savings and new contributions. Total interest reflects investment gains before any taxable-account tax, while Total tax accumulates the modeled annual taxes on positive taxable-account growth. These values reconcile to the projected balance, subject to whole-dollar rounding.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are qualified Roth IRA withdrawals tax-free?

Generally, qualified Roth IRA distributions can be tax-free when applicable age and five-year requirements are met. This calculator models tax-free Roth growth but does not decide whether a particular withdrawal is qualified.

Do both accounts use the same annual investment return?

Yes. Before taxes, both accounts use the same yearly return. Their balances differ because the regular taxable account is modeled as paying taxes on positive earnings each year.

Can I contribute the annual maximum for every future year?

Actual yearly limits and your ability to contribute may change. Maximize mode is an illustrative estimate using 2026 limits, not a promise that those amounts will remain available or that you qualify to contribute.

Does the calculator account for inflation or actual capital-gains tax rules?

No. Results are shown in nominal dollars, and the taxable account uses a simplified annual earnings-tax model. Real-world taxation depends on dividends, interest, capital gains, holding periods, and individual circumstances.
