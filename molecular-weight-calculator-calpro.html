Molecular Weight Formula

The molar mass of a compound is the sum of each constituent element's atomic weight multiplied by the number of atoms of that element in the formula:

M = Σ(ni × Ai)

Here, M is molar mass in grams per mole (g/mol), ni is the number of atoms of element i, and Ai is that element's abridged atomic weight. Percent composition by mass equals (ni × Ai) ÷ M × 100%. Atomic composition equals ni ÷ (total atoms) × 100%.

Example 1: Water (H2O)

Water contains two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. Using H = 1.008 and O = 15.999, its molar mass is 2 × 1.008 + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol. Hydrogen accounts for approximately 11.19% of its mass and 66.67% of its atoms; oxygen accounts for 88.81% of its mass and 33.33% of its atoms.

Example 2: Aluminum Sulfate, Al2(SO4)3

The group (SO4) occurs three times, giving 2 Al atoms, 3 S atoms, and 12 O atoms. The calculation is 2 × 26.982 + 3 × 32.06 + 12 × 15.999 = 342.132 g/mol.

Example 3: Copper(II) Sulfate Pentahydrate

The formula CuSO4·5H2O uses a dot to indicate five water units per formula unit. The calculator accepts either a middle dot or a regular period (CuSO4.5H2O). The molar mass is 249.677 g/mol using the abridged values supplied.

Understanding the Results

The Atom column identifies each element; Count shows its total number of atoms; Atomic Weight shows the abridged reference value; and Mass Subtotal shows that element's contribution to the compound's total molar mass. The atomic-composition chart visualizes percentages by number of atoms, whereas the mass-composition chart uses percentages by mass. These two distributions will usually differ.

The calculation uses abridged elemental weights, so the displayed result is appropriate for general chemistry calculations but is not an exact isotopic mass or a measurement of a specific sample.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are molecular weight and molar mass the same?

They have related numerical values in ordinary chemistry calculations, but strictly speaking relative molecular mass is dimensionless, whereas molar mass is expressed in g/mol. This calculator reports the numerical result as molar mass in g/mol.

Can I use parentheses, square brackets, or curly braces?

Yes. Multipliers and nested groups are supported, including Al2(SO4)3, [Co(NH3)6]Cl3, and K4[Fe(CN)6]. Whole-number hydrate coefficients are also supported.

Why does capitalization matter?

Element symbols are case-sensitive. For example, Co is cobalt, while CO contains one carbon atom and one oxygen atom. An incorrectly lowercase formula such as h2o is rejected.

Can I enter isotope notation, ionic charges, or fractional atom counts?

The calculator supports ordinary stoichiometric formulas with positive whole-number counts. Explicit isotope labels, ionic-charge suffixes, and fractional stoichiometries are outside its scope. Use an uncharged formula unit when calculating the molar mass of a salt.

Why might another source give a slightly different result?

Atomic weights may be given at different rounding precisions and can vary slightly with isotopic composition. For elements lacking a standard atomic weight, the source table uses a representative mass number rather than a sample-specific atomic weight. The calculator ignores published measurement uncertainty.
