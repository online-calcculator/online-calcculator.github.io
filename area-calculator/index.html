Area Calculator

Area measures how much two-dimensional space a shape covers. For a flat room, plot, patio, or drawing, it tells you the size of the region inside the boundary, not the distance around it. Area is written in square units, such as square feet (ft²), square inches (in²), or square meters (m²). This guide covers seven familiar shapes, each with the measurements needed, the formula, and an independently checked example. Complex outlines can often be divided into smaller, recognizable pieces.

How to use the area calculator

  1. Choose the shape that matches your drawing. The matching panel uses these dimensions: rectangle: length and width; triangle: three edge lengths; trapezoid: two parallel bases and perpendicular height; circle: radius; sector: radius and central angle; ellipse: semi-major and semi-minor axes; parallelogram: base and perpendicular height.
  2. Enter the measured values and select appropriate units where provided.

Keep dimensions consistent before applying a formula. If the shape has an angle setting, check whether the angle is in degrees or radians. An area answer represents the enclosed flat region and uses squared length units. It does not automatically include a material allowance, thickness, or the perimeter of the boundary.

Rectangle: length × width

A rectangle is a four-sided polygon (quadrilateral) with four right angles. Its opposite sides are equal in length and parallel. A square is the special case in which length and width are the same.

Formula: A = l × w. Here A is area, l is length, and w is width. Think of the rectangle as rows of unit squares: the number along one side multiplied by the number along the other side gives the total number of squares. Both measurements must use the same length unit.

Example: a rectangular bedroom measures 18 ft by 12 ft. Substitute the dimensions: A = 18 × 12 = 216 ft². That is 216 square feet of floor surface, before accounting for any cutouts or installation waste.

Triangle: three sides and Heron’s formula

A triangle has three straight sides joining three vertices. This calculator's triangle panel takes Edge 1 (a), Edge 2 (b), and Edge 3 (c), so the relevant method is Heron’s formula. You do not need to measure the altitude to apply it.

For a valid, nondegenerate triangle, each pair of sides must add to more than the remaining side: a + b > c, a + c > b, and b + c > a. Equality would make a collapsed, zero-area boundary rather than an ordinary triangle.

Formula, step 1: s = (a + b + c) / 2, where s is the semiperimeter, half the distance around the triangle. Formula, step 2: A = √[s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)]. All three edges must be in the same unit, and the result is in that unit squared.

If you know a base and its perpendicular altitude instead, the alternative formula is A = (base × height) / 2. The altitude is a right-angle distance to the chosen base (or its extension), not necessarily another side.

Example: a triangular sign has edge lengths of 13 cm, 14 cm, and 15 cm. First, s = (13 + 14 + 15) / 2 = 21 cm. Then A = √[21 × (21 − 13) × (21 − 14) × (21 − 15)] = √(21 × 8 × 7 × 6) = √7,056 = 84 cm². The triangle inequality holds for these measurements.

Why the method works: the three sides fix the triangle’s shape (up to reflection), so the altitude is determined even when you have not measured it. Heron’s formula packages that geometry into side lengths alone.

Trapezoid: average the parallel bases

A trapezoid is a quadrilateral with a pair of parallel sides called bases, b₁ and b₂. The two other sides are legs. The formula applies to a simple trapezoid when h is the perpendicular distance between its parallel bases; a diagonal or slanted leg is not h. Some textbooks use “at least one pair” and others “exactly one pair” of parallel sides, but the area formula is unchanged.

Formula: A = [(b₁ + b₂) / 2] × h. Averaging the parallel base lengths gives the effective width; multiplying by the perpendicular height gives area. You can also understand this by joining two matching trapezoids to create a parallelogram.

Example: a trapezoidal planter is 12 ft along its bottom, 8 ft along its top, and 5 ft high. A = [(12 + 8) / 2] × 5 = 10 × 5 = 50 ft². If the sloping leg were 5 ft but the perpendicular height differed, this 50 ft² answer would not be valid.

Circle: area from the radius

A circle consists of all points in a plane at a fixed distance from its center. That distance is the radius r; the diameter d reaches across the circle through its center, so d = 2r.

Formula: A = πr², where π ≈ 3.14159265. Squaring the radius is essential: if you double r, the enclosed area becomes four times as large, because both horizontal and vertical dimensions scale.

Example: a round garden has a radius of 4 m. A = π × 4² = 16π ≈ 50.265 m². If a measurement of 8 m refers to the diameter, first divide it by 2 rather than using 8 as the radius.

Circular sector: a portion of a disk

A circular sector is the part of a disk enclosed between two radii and the arc connecting their endpoints, like a wedge. Its area depends on radius r and the central angle θ. A full revolution is 360° or 2π radians.

When θ is in degrees, A = (θ / 360°) × πr². When θ is in radians, A = ½r²θ. The formulas agree because 180° = π radians. Do not substitute a degree value into the radian equation.

Example: a circular section of lawn has radius 5 m and angle 72°. A = (72 / 360) × π × 5² = 0.2 × 25π = 5π ≈ 15.708 m². The same angle is 2π/5 radians, giving A = ½ × 25 × (2π/5) = 5π m².

For a single sector of a disk, its central angle is generally between 0° and 360° (inclusive). A sector with θ = 360° is the whole disk; θ = 0° encloses zero area.

Ellipse: the two semi-axes

An ellipse is a closed oval-shaped curve characterized by two fixed points called foci, F₁ and F₂. For any point P on the curve, PF₁ + PF₂ is constant. When the two foci coincide, the ellipse becomes a circle.

The major axis is the ellipse’s longest diameter, and the minor axis is its shortest diameter. The semi-major axis a and semi-minor axis b reach from the center to the boundary, so the full diameters are 2a and 2b. For the usual labels a ≥ b > 0. If you measure the complete width and height, divide each by two before entering the semi-axis lengths.

Formula: A = πab. A circle uses two equal radii; an ellipse stretches one radius independently in each perpendicular direction, so its area follows the product of the two semi-axis lengths. The distance from the center to either focus is c = √(a² − b²), which is useful for understanding the shape but is not needed to calculate its area.

Example: an oval path encloses an ellipse whose semi-major axis is 6 ft and semi-minor axis is 4 ft. A = π × 6 × 4 = 24π ≈ 75.398 ft². If those numbers were the complete major and minor axis lengths instead, the area would be only π × 3 × 2 = 6π ft².

Parallelogram: base × perpendicular height

A parallelogram is a quadrilateral with two pairs of parallel opposite sides. Opposite sides are equal, and opposite angles are equal. Rectangles, rhombuses, and squares are all special kinds of parallelograms.

Formula: A = b × h, with b the chosen base and h the perpendicular distance to the opposite, parallel side. The sloping side is not normally the height. Cut a triangle from one end of a slanted parallelogram and shift it to the other end: the pieces form a rectangle with the same base and height, without changing area.

Example: a tile pattern has a base of 11 cm and perpendicular height of 4 cm. A = 11 × 4 = 44 cm². Measuring a 5 cm slanted edge in place of the 4 cm height would incorrectly give 55 cm².

Measuring an irregular or composite area

The seven formulas describe idealized simple shapes. For an L-shaped floor, a garden with a circular bed, or a plan containing openings, sketch the outline and divide it into non-overlapping rectangles, triangles, and other measurable pieces. Calculate each piece separately, add the areas that belong to the region, and subtract holes, cutouts, or excluded regions. Do not add overlapping parts twice.

Example: an L-shaped room can be viewed as a 10 ft × 8 ft outer rectangle with a 4 ft × 3 ft corner missing. The area is (10 × 8) − (4 × 3) = 80 − 12 = 68 ft². This subtraction approach is often faster and less error-prone than measuring several separate rectangles.

For a very irregular or curved boundary, approximating it with many smaller shapes produces an estimate, not necessarily an exact value. Surveyed parcels and construction purchases may need more precise boundary data, allowances for cuts or waste, and professional measurement; a geometric area does not by itself establish a legal property boundary.

Common area units and conversion

Area units are squared length units. For instance, 1 ft = 0.3048 m exactly, so 1 ft² = (0.3048)² m² = 0.09290304 m², not 0.3048 m². To convert a computed area, multiply by the corresponding factor below. The values for the international foot, acre, and mile follow published NIST conventions.

Measurement and interpretation checks

First identify the actual geometric quantity: area covers the inside, perimeter measures the boundary, and volume measures three-dimensional space. Record length dimensions in consistent units, then square those units in the answer.

💡 Tip: Check the specific shape inputs before calculating. A circle needs radius rather than diameter; an ellipse needs semi-axes rather than full axes; the triangle-by-three-sides method requires three feasible sides; trapezoids and parallelograms use perpendicular, not slanted, height. A sector’s angle unit must match its formula.

Physical measurements are approximate. If an edge is measured to the nearest inch or centimeter, reporting many decimal places in its area does not make it more precise. Use sufficient intermediate precision and round the final answer to match the quality of the measurements.
