Bits, bytes, bandwidth, and throughput

A bit (b) is one binary digit; a byte (B) is eight bits. Data storage and file sizes are commonly reported in bytes (MB, GB), while Internet connection rates are commonly reported in bits per second (Mbps, Gbps). The letter case matters: 100 Mbps means 100 megabits per second, not 100 megabytes per second. Dividing 100 by 8 gives a theoretical maximum of 12.5 MB/s before transfer overhead.

Bandwidth is a capacity or stated data rate. Throughput is the rate a transfer actually achieves. TCP/IP headers, encryption, retransmissions, congestion, Wi-Fi conditions, storage limits, and server capacity can make useful throughput lower than the advertised line rate. Download and upload rates can also differ. A 100 Mbps advertised connection does not guarantee a 100 Mbps file download.

Decimal versus binary prefixes

The SI prefixes kilo, mega, and giga multiply by powers of 1,000; binary prefixes kibi, mebi, and gibi use powers of 1,024. Use KB/MB/GB for decimal byte quantities and KiB/MiB/GiB for binary quantities when the source makes that distinction. Some software labels binary values with decimal-looking abbreviations, so confirm the convention when exact transfer totals matter.

Worked conversion: 2.5 GB to MB and gigabits

With decimal units, 2.5 GB = 2.5 × 1,000 = 2,500 MB. Since each byte is eight bits, 2.5 GB × 8 = 20 Gb (gigabits). This conversion changes the data amount, not the time required to move it. A value in gigabits is not automatically a rate in Gbps.

Estimate download or upload time

Enter File Size with its storage unit and Bandwidth with its rate unit. Convert file size to bits, then divide by the rate in bits per second. The same equation works for upload and download; what changes is the applicable direction’s speed. If the interface reports a time in seconds, divide by 60 for minutes or by 3,600 for hours.

Transfer time (seconds) = file size (bytes) × 8 ÷ data rate (bits/second).

Worked example: a 2 GB file on an 80 Mbps connection

Assume a decimal 2 GB file and a steady, usable 80 Mbps transfer rate. Step 1: 2 GB = 2,000,000,000 bytes. Step 2: × 8 = 16,000,000,000 bits. Step 3: ÷ 80,000,000 bits/second = 200 seconds, or 3 minutes 20 seconds. This is an idealized duration, not a promise. At an actual payload rate of 64 Mbps, the same file takes 250 seconds (4 minutes 10 seconds), ignoring any setup delay.

What makes a real transfer take longer?

A practical transfer is limited by the slowest relevant part of the path, not just your subscription’s headline speed. Concurrent downloads, server throttling, packet loss and retries, high round-trip time with constrained TCP windows, and disk-write speed can matter. For cloud backups, choose the upload speed, not the download speed. If you know a measured sustained throughput, use that as the bandwidth input for a more realistic estimate.

Estimate website data transfer

Website bandwidth planning starts with how many page views are served and how many bytes are transferred per page view. In the Website Bandwidth Calculator, enter Page Views and choose the displayed period, enter Average Page Size and its unit, and specify a Redundancy Factor. Include real visitors, crawlers, assets not served from cache, APIs, and other traffic that your hosting provider actually counts.

Estimated transfer volume = page views × average transferred bytes per page view × redundancy factor.

Here, page views means completed page requests in the selected period; average page size should represent bytes transferred across all relevant page resources, not merely the HTML document. The redundancy factor is an explicit multiplier for additional requests, retransfers, traffic uncertainty, or planning headroom. A factor of 1 adds no extra allowance; 1.3 adds 30%. Do not automatically add the same bot traffic both to page views and again through the factor.

Worked example: 75,000 monthly page views

Suppose a site serves 75,000 page views per month, each averaging 1.8 MB of transferred resources. Base monthly traffic is 75,000 × 1.8 MB = 135,000 MB = 135 GB. With an illustrative redundancy factor of 1.25, the planning allowance becomes 168.75 GB per month. The extra 33.75 GB is a planning margin, not measured bot traffic. If the page-view input is daily rather than monthly, convert to a monthly period before comparing with a monthly hosting quota.

Data allowance is not peak server capacity

A monthly GB figure measures volume, not how quickly the server must deliver pages during busy minutes. For capacity planning, also estimate simultaneous requests, response sizes, caching and content delivery network (CDN) offload, and bursts. Hosting plans may count ingress and egress separately and may exclude or include CDN delivery; consult the provider’s metering rules.

Convert monthly hosting traffic to Mbps

Hosting packages often quote a monthly transfer allowance in GB or TB, while a network port is described in Mbps or Gbps. These are different quantities: volume and rate. An equivalent average rate answers how fast data would need to flow continuously to use a stated transfer volume over the selected month. It does not indicate the peak rate required when visits are concentrated in shorter periods.

Average rate (Mbps) = monthly transfer (GB) × 8,000 ÷ seconds in the month. This expression assumes decimal GB, decimal Mbps, and one byte = eight bits.

Inverse: monthly transfer (GB) = average rate (Mbps) × seconds in the month ÷ 8,000.

Worked example: 1,000 GB per month

With a 30-day illustrative month, 1,000 GB × 8,000 ÷ 2,592,000 seconds = 3.08642 Mbps of continuous average transfer. A convention based on 365.25 days ÷ 12 months = 30.4375 days gives 3.04206 Mbps instead. Both are mathematically correct under different assumptions; a specific converter’s displayed value depends on its month-length convention. A true 31-day billing window gives a different result again.

At a steady 10 Mbps for every second of a 30-day month, the corresponding decimal volume is 10 × 2,592,000 ÷ 8,000 = 3,240 GB (3.24 TB). Actual metered totals may differ because a service is rarely continuously saturated and provider billing rules vary.

Mobile technologies: avoid interpreting a generation as one fixed speed

Cellular networks are especially variable. A 4G or 5G label does not identify a single guaranteed download or upload rate; spectrum, radio conditions, device capabilities, network configuration, signal congestion, and uplink/downlink direction all matter. Standards-oriented peak figures describe idealized capabilities or evaluation requirements, not what an individual customer will receive.

Frequent mistakes and important limits

Confusing Mbps with MB/s introduces an eightfold error. Mixing GB with GiB changes the number of bytes in the file. Treating average monthly Mbps as sufficient peak capacity hides busy-hour demand. Counting total page weight instead of transferred bytes can misstate cache and CDN effects. Treating a nominal wireless or Ethernet link rate as achieved payload throughput overstates transfer performance.

💡 Tip: Use nonnegative data volumes and rates. A zero bandwidth with a positive file size cannot produce a finite transfer time; a zero-byte file has zero ideal data-transfer time but may still incur connection overhead. Negative file sizes, page views, or redundancy factors do not represent meaningful traffic. Extremely small or large values may require scientific notation or rounded outputs.
